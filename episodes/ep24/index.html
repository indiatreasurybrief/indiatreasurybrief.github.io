Repatriation from India: how MNCs move cash home as rates rise

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Rupee & FX · Regulation

Sooner or later, a foreign parent asks its Indian company to send cash home. Coca-Cola's Indian bottler plans to list by selling only shares its Singapore company owns, so none of the money goes to the bottler. Most subsidiaries never list, so which route does their cash take, and what does each one cost?

We cover why the parent wants the cash now, and the routes home by kind of money: profit as a dividend, with withholding tax and treaty rates; fees, royalties and parent loans; import advances; and capital through a buyback, a reduction of capital or a sale of shares. Then the case for and against sending it, and five checks I'd run before anyone promises the parent a number, with a note for startups that have a foreign investor.

Sooner or later, a foreign parent asks its Indian company to send cash home. So which route gets it there, and what does each cost? Coca-Cola has picked an unusual one. Its Indian bottler plans to list on the stock market by selling shares that Coca-Cola's Singapore company owns, so the money goes to Singapore and not a rupee goes to the bottler. Most companies never list, so the money has to come out of their own bank accounts. And with borrowing dearer in India and in America, I'd say that cash is harder for the parent to ignore.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for India's treasury and finance teams, from big corporates to startups.

I'll cover three things. The first is why the parent wants the cash, and where Coca-Cola's sale fits. The second is the routes home, which depend on what the money is. And the third is what I'd weigh before the subsidiary agrees to send it.

So, first, why the parent wants the cash.

One reason is interest rates. In September, America's Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by twenty five basis points. That put it between three point seven five and four per cent. When it held the rate in October, both members who voted against wanted another rise. The RBI's monetary policy committee raised the repo rate on the seventh of October, its first rise in nearly four years, and set it at five point five per cent. Both meet again in December, the RBI on the fourth and the Fed on the ninth.

A group that borrows at floating rates pays more on its loans than it did in August. I'd say that makes the cash sitting in India more useful at home, where it could pay down some of those loans.

Some groups have already chosen to pay out more. Three M India, Nestlé India and Linde India all announced special dividends in twenty twenty six, but none of them said rates were the reason, and I won't guess at one.

Coca-Cola's reason is different. Business Standard reports that the sale is part of a global plan by Coca-Cola to step back from running bottling operations.

The routes most subsidiaries use rest on one distinction. The cash in an Indian bank account isn't one pot of money. To the parent, some of it is profit, some is money it's owed as a fee or a loan, and some is capital it put in. Each kind has its own way home.

So, second, the routes home, starting with profit.

Profit goes home as a dividend, and it's the route most subsidiaries use. The Companies Act lets a company pay dividends only out of profits, from the current year or from past years if they haven't been paid out. Nestlé India's special dividend in July was paid from its retained earnings. Old losses have to be set off first. Unrealised gains don't count, and nor does fresh capital from the parent or cash that customers paid in advance. So a full bank account can mean a small dividend.

The board can declare an interim dividend at any point in the year, and the final dividend is declared at the annual general meeting. Some companies declare more than one interim, and Colgate-Palmolive India declared them in May and again in October.

Next comes the tax. The subsidiary withholds tax on the dividend at a basic rate of twenty per cent, and the tax treaty with the parent's country usually cuts that rate. For an American parent, the treaty rate is fifteen per cent. For a Singapore parent with at least a quarter of the shares, it's ten.

Other shareholders also cut into what the parent gets. A dividend goes to every shareholder, so if the subsidiary is listed or has a joint venture partner, only the parent's share goes abroad. Before tax, a parent with sixty per cent of the shares gets sixty rupees of every hundred paid out.

Once a dividend is declared, the money has to go into a separate bank account within five days. The subsidiary then has thirty days from the declaration to pay it, so I'd have the treaty papers and the bank's forms ready before the board meets.

So, in short, a dividend can only come out of profit, and withholding tax usually takes between ten and twenty per cent.

The second kind of money is what the subsidiary owes the parent. The parent may provide a real service, license its brand or its technology, or lend the subsidiary money. The subsidiary pays for each one as what it is: a fee, a royalty or a loan repayment.

These payments are also taxed. The subsidiary withholds tax on fees, royalties and interest, and the treaty usually lowers the rate. The difference is that a genuine fee or royalty is a business expense, so it comes out of income before tax. A dividend comes out of profit after tax.

Each one also needs its own checks. A fee has to be for a service that was actually provided, at a price an unrelated company would pay, or the tax department can disallow the expense.

A listed subsidiary has one more step. Under SEBI's listing rules, large royalty or brand payments to the parent need the approval of shareholders, and the parent itself can't vote.

A loan from the parent is the simplest of the three. The subsidiary repays it through its bank as the agreement sets out, and tax falls on the interest, not on the repayment. But the loan counts as external commercial borrowing, and those rules set a minimum average maturity, so the subsidiary can't always repay early.

A subsidiary that imports goods from its parent can also pay for them in advance. Under the FEMA rules in force from October, the contract sets the delivery date, and if the goods never arrive, the advance has to come back. I'd see this as changing when the cash moves, not how much.

The third kind of money is capital, which the parent put in when the subsidiary was set up or as it grew. It can be sent back in one of three ways.

The first way is a buyback, where the subsidiary buys back some of its own shares. The tax on it changed on the first of April, and before that, buyback money was taxed as a dividend. The Finance Act made it a capital gain, so the parent is taxed only on its profit over what it paid for the shares.

But the Act also added an extra tax for promoters. For a promoter that's an Indian company, the extra tax on long term gains is nine point five per cent. For any other promoter, and that includes a foreign parent, it's seventeen point five per cent. Added to the usual capital gains tax of twelve point five per cent, a foreign parent pays about thirty per cent of its gain, before surcharge and any treaty relief.

Several tax summaries give twenty two per cent as the rate for a company promoter, but that figure applies only to an Indian company. And in an unlisted company, the Act treats anyone holding more than ten per cent as a promoter.

The second way is a reduction of capital, where the subsidiary cancels part of its share capital and pays the money back. It needs the company law tribunal's approval, so the tribunal sets the date. And it isn't automatically tax free. Up to the profits the subsidiary has built up, the payout is taxed as a dividend.

The third way is the one Coca-Cola picked: the parent sells some of its own shares. It can sell them privately to another investor, or in a listing, where it's called an offer for sale. Either way, the buyer pays the parent directly. The subsidiary's own cash is never touched.

A reduction of capital and a listing are both intensive processes that can each take several months, so neither suits cash the parent needs soon.

Whichever route the money takes, a little more is lost when it changes currency. The bank converts the rupees at its own rate, and its margin and charges come out of what the parent receives. The margin is usually far smaller than the tax, but on a large payment, I'd ask more than one bank for a quote.

So, third, what I'd weigh before the subsidiary agrees to send the cash.

The first question is whether to send the cash at all, and the case for sending it rests on one comparison. On one side is what the cash earns in India, after any fall in the rupee, and on the other is what the parent pays to borrow.

The rupee matters because it has tended to fall. Since November twenty twenty one, it has lost about a fifth of its value against the dollar. But the fall isn't steady. The rupee hit a record low near ninety seven per dollar in October and was back under ninety four by the middle of November. So the dollars the parent receives depend on when the money crosses.

The case against has three parts. The first is that Indian rates rose too, so the cash can earn more where it sits. The second is tax, because most routes lose some of the money on the way. And the third is that a subsidiary that sends its cash home and later needs it will have to borrow it back at the higher rates.

That decision belongs to the parent and to you. Whichever way it goes, I'd run five checks before anyone promises a number.

The first check is to sort the cash. I'd set aside what the business needs to keep, and then split the rest into profit it can pay out, money owed to the parent, and capital.

The second is to read the loan agreements. Term loans often limit dividends and other payouts while they're outstanding, so a lender may need to agree before the parent is promised anything.

The third is to work out the net figure, not the gross. For a dividend, that means the parent's share after withholding tax at the treaty rate, and for a buyback, it means what's left after the promoter's extra tax.

The fourth is to set a date the approvals allow. An interim dividend needs only the board. A large buyback needs a shareholders' vote, and a listed subsidiary also goes through SEBI's process. And a reduction of capital waits on the tribunal.

And the fifth is to put all of it on one page for the parent: each route, what leaves the subsidiary, what reaches the parent, the date, and who signs.

If you run finance at a startup or a smaller company with a foreign investor, the picture is simpler and stricter. Money from a funding round isn't profit, so it can't go back as a dividend. An investor who wants money out usually sells its shares to someone else rather than taking cash from the company. The paperwork for smaller payments abroad is also lighter, because a chartered accountant's certificate isn't needed until taxable payments in a tax year pass five lakh rupees.

And if your company is the Indian branch of a foreign company rather than a subsidiary, its rules are different, and this episode doesn't cover them.

The RBI decides on the fourth of December, and the Fed on the ninth. Either decision could change what the cash is worth where it sits. But what the cash is won't change, and neither will the route it takes home.

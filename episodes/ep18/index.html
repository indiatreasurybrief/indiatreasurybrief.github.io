Dollar loan vs rupee loan: what an ECB really costs, and the GIFT City route

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Rates · Rupee & FX

Borrowing in rupees got more expensive in October, and the RBI is making dollar loans cheaper, but only for PSUs and banks. This episode looks at how a dollar loan (an ECB, or external commercial borrowing) is priced, how it compares with a rupee loan, and whether it's a good idea for your company.

We go through what changed this year: the ECB cost ceiling removed in February, the RBI's 1.5% hedge for PSU and bank borrowing from June, and the rate moves in India and the US. Then the price, piece by piece: the SOFR benchmark, the lender's margin, the cost of the hedge, and the withholding tax that GIFT City removes. Once it's hedged, a dollar loan usually costs about what a rupee loan costs. Unhedged, it's a bet on the rupee, unless the company earns dollars. Then what I'd do, for a large company, a PSU, an exporter, and a smaller company or startup.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

This month, borrowing in rupees got more expensive. The Reserve Bank raised its rate at the start of October, and within a few weeks, a big company selling three-month commercial paper was paying nearly seven per cent, against about six per cent a month earlier. At the same time, the Reserve Bank has been making dollar loans much cheaper, but only for some borrowers. Since June, a public sector company or a bank that borrows dollars abroad can protect itself against the rupee with the Reserve Bank itself, at a fixed hedge cost of one point five per cent per year. Everyone else pays roughly twice that for the same protection. And that difference decides whether a dollar loan makes sense for the rest of us.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

So in this episode, we'll look at dollar loans. First, we'll see how a dollar loan is priced. Second, we'll compare it with a rupee loan. And third, we'll ask whether it's a good idea for our own company.

Before we get to the price, I think it's worth knowing what has changed, because quite a lot has, and it's all happened in the last nine months.

First, the rules got simpler. When an Indian company borrows dollars from abroad, the Reserve Bank calls it an external commercial borrowing, or E-C-B. Until February, there was a ceiling on how much interest we could pay on one of these loans. In February, the Reserve Bank removed that ceiling, so the price is now whatever the lender and the borrower agree. It also raised the limit on this kind of borrowing. It's now a billion dollars, or three times a company's net worth if that's higher.

Second, the Reserve Bank started paying for the hedge, for public sector companies and banks. In June, with the rupee under pressure, it opened a window for them. If a public sector company takes a dollar loan of three years or more, or a bank borrows dollars abroad, it can swap those dollars into rupees with the Reserve Bank at that fixed one point five per cent per year. The window is open for new loans until the end of December. And so far, the take-up on the loan side has been modest. By the middle of August, public sector companies had brought in about one point seven billion dollars of these loans through the window, and banks close to three billion. Across all companies, dollar loans into India were modest in August too, at a net inflow of about one point six billion dollars, according to the Reserve Bank.

Third, the price of money moved on both sides. In India, the Reserve Bank raised its repo rate to five point five per cent in October, and we've seen what that did to commercial paper. In America, the Federal Reserve held its rate at the end of October, at between three point seven five and four per cent, but two of its members wanted to raise it.

So the question I keep hearing is a simple one. If rupee money is costlier and the rules are easier, should a company that isn't a P-S-U be borrowing in dollars again?

To answer that, let's start with how a dollar loan is priced. I think it helps to see it as four pieces stacked on top of each other, and I'll use a large Indian company with a top credit rating as the example.

The first piece is the benchmark. Most dollar loans now start from a rate called SOFR, which is what it costs to borrow dollars overnight in America against government bonds, and it's a little under four per cent at the moment.

The second piece is the lender's margin, and that depends on how much the lender trusts the borrower. Indian banks have recently been paying a little over one per cent on top of SOFR, and a strong company would pay something similar. So far, that's about five per cent per year.

The third piece is the hedge. If our income is in rupees, we have to protect ourselves against the rupee falling before the loan is repaid, and for a loan of three years or more, that protection costs about three per cent per year right now. That takes the loan to about eight per cent. And it isn't an accident that the hedge costs this much, because its price mostly reflects the gap between Indian and American interest rates.

The fourth piece is tax, and this is where it matters where we book the loan. When an Indian company pays interest to a lender abroad, it normally has to hold back tax on that interest, usually somewhere between a tenth and a fifth of it, depending on which country the lender is in. In practice, lenders usually price that tax into the loan, so it's the borrower who ends up paying it, and on a loan like this, that can add as much as a percentage point.

There's a way round it, though, and it's GIFT City, India's international finance centre in Gujarat. Indian and foreign banks run what are called banking units there, and they lend in dollars. In July, the tax department confirmed that most interest paid to an eligible bank in GIFT City can be paid without holding back that tax. I think that's a big part of why, last year, about two-thirds of all the dollar loans Indian companies raised went through GIFT City, according to Reserve Bank data. The year before, it was about a third.

So how does that compare with a rupee loan? For our large company, a hedged dollar loan booked through GIFT City costs about eight per cent. In rupees, the same company pays somewhere between seven point five and eight per cent on a three-year bond. So the dollar loan costs about the same, or a little more, and if it's booked outside GIFT City, it costs clearly more.

Now take a mid-sized company, one that borrows from its bank at, say, around ten per cent. Foreign lenders charge it a bigger margin than they charge a top company, often between two and three percentage points over SOFR. Once it adds the same hedge, it ends up at about ten per cent as well. So once again, it's roughly a tie, and the tax can tip it either way.

I think that's the heart of it. Once the loan is hedged, a dollar loan usually costs about what a rupee loan costs, because the saving on the interest rate goes back out in the hedge.

So why not skip the hedge? Without it, the dollar loan does look between two and four percentage points cheaper per year. But the rupee averaged about eighty eight to the dollar in the last financial year, and it's averaged about ninety five in this one. That's a fall of about eight per cent, and it would have wiped out two or three years of that saving in a single year. So for a company that earns in rupees, I'd say an unhedged dollar loan isn't really a cheaper loan. It's a bet on the rupee.

The exception is a company that earns dollars. An exporter, or a services company that bills clients abroad, already has dollars coming in, and those dollars can repay the loan. For that company, an unhedged or part-hedged loan, sized to the dollars it actually earns, can genuinely be cheaper.

And the other exception is the one the Reserve Bank built. A public sector company that hedges with the Reserve Bank at one point five per cent ends up paying about six point five per cent, well below what it would pay in rupees. So the cheap dollar is real, but at the moment, it's been built for the borrowers the Reserve Bank wants dollars from.

So is a dollar loan a good idea for our own company? Here's what I'd do, and I'll take the large company first.

First, I'd compare the hedged cost of the dollar loan with our own rupee options, which means our bond, our commercial paper and our bank lines, and not with the headline dollar rate. That's the comparison that decides it.

Second, if the dollar loan still wins, I'd ask a bank in GIFT City first, because of the tax, and I'd decide how much to hedge before we sign, not after the rupee has moved.

Third, if we're a public sector company, I'd look at the Reserve Bank's window now, because new loans have to be drawn by the end of December.

And if we're an exporter, I'd size any dollar borrowing to the dollars we actually earn, and hedge the rest.

Now, if we're a smaller company or a startup, I think the honest answer is that a dollar loan is open to us, but it rarely pays. A manufacturer can borrow as much as a hundred and fifty million dollars this way, for as little as a year, which is more flexible than it used to be. But for a business that earns in rupees, the hedge usually takes away most of the saving, and the margins foreign lenders charge smaller borrowers are higher. So I'd check with a GIFT City bank what its minimum loan size is, and I'd compare the full cost honestly with what our own bank offers. And if we're a startup with dollar revenue, say a software company billing clients abroad, that changes the picture, and the exporter's logic applies to us too.

So will dollar loans get cheaper for the rest of us? I don't think anyone can say for sure, and there are forces both ways. If the Reserve Bank opens its cheap hedge to private companies, or keeps it running past December, the answer could change quickly. If the Federal Reserve raises rates in December, dollar loans get more expensive, but the hedge would get a little cheaper, so the two partly cancel out. And if the rupee keeps sliding, the unhedged loan stays a bet. The one thing I'd watch is whether the Reserve Bank widens that window, because that's the change that would turn a tie into a real saving.

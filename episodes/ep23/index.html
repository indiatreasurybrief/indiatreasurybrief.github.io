Can treasury run without email? What the Microsoft outage exposed

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Tech & operations

Could your treasury keep moving money without its inbox? Microsoft's email outage ran across the twelfth and thirteenth of November, and users in more than seventy countries reported problems.

This is a special edition, an extra episode alongside our regular Wednesday and Saturday releases. We look at where email sits in treasury work: approvals, supporting documents and bank responses. We cover which activities deserve an agreed alternative, including an overseas receipt that needs documents before the cash can be used, and how to use the bank platforms already available, with onboarding done before the next outage. For a startup, the same risk can sit with one person.

Microsoft's November email outage disrupted businesses around the world. Could your treasury keep money moving without its inbox? Your bank portal might still work, but an overseas payment could need documents, or a loan could need an instruction. If your team handles those steps by email, logging in won't finish the job. And an online alternative is only useful if your company is already set up to use it.

This is a special edition of the India Treasury Brief, an extra episode alongside our regular Wednesday and Saturday releases. It's for India's treasury and finance teams, from big corporates to startups.

I'll cover three things. Where email sits in treasury work, which activities deserve a backup, and how to make the available alternatives useful.

The outage ran across the twelfth and thirteenth of November. Users in more than seventy countries reported problems. Microsoft apologised, and it says the cause was a technical fault and not a cyber attack. Reuters reported that a financial firm in Taiwan cancelled a bond repo, and Bloomberg reported traders going back to phones and fax. In India, IndiGo and Akasa Air said their email support was affected.

The payment systems themselves don't run on email, and I know of no report of one failing in India. So the answer to that opening question is mostly yes, and the trouble sits around the payment.

First, the dependency is bigger than sending an instruction.

Email connects people, decisions and information across a treasury team. Some of that work happens before anyone opens the bank portal, and some continues after a payment has been submitted.

An approval might sit in one inbox, the supporting documents in another, and the bank's response in a shared mailbox. Each person may be able to access their own system while the team still cannot finish the job.

So access to the bank is only one part of being able to transact. The team also needs the approval, the documents and a way to reach the bank.

And the outage can reach beyond our team. A supplier, group company or bank contact may be unable to respond even when our own systems are working.

An outage of this scale is unusual, and I'd keep the preparation proportionate. We cannot prepare for every possible failure, or build a separate backup for everything the treasury team does.

The useful starting point is the work that cannot wait. I'd identify the activities whose interruption could leave an important obligation unpaid, funding unavailable, or the team unsure how much cash it can use.

Second, I'd decide which activities deserve an agreed alternative.

I'd look at three kinds of dependency: getting a decision, completing a transaction, and knowing what has happened to the cash. Together, they show how a communication problem can affect the decisions we make and the obligations we need to meet.

The decision comes first. If an internal approval exists only in an email chain, a working bank portal does not resolve the missing authority.

The same applies to documents. A team may know that an invoice or agreement exists, yet be unable to retrieve the version needed to act.

These are internal dependencies worth recognising alongside the bank's requirements. Moving an instruction online may help, while leaving the approval and supporting information tied to the inbox.

Then there's the transaction itself. With an overseas receipt, money reaching the bank does not always mean the company can immediately use it.

Citi's service guide for India says a service export receipt is credited the same day only when the documents and the disposal instruction are complete.

Where a company supplies those by email, the communication becomes part of getting access to the cash. That distinction matters most when the company is relying on the receipt to meet another payment due that day.

The same can hold for the documents on an overseas payment, for accepting a deposit rate, and for a loan drawdown instruction. Each depends on the bank, the product and what the company has agreed, so I'd check our own.

A bank may also seek clarification before completing a transaction. A response to a fraud verification or screening query could be delayed even while the underlying checking system continues to work.

The third dependency is information. Treasury needs to know what has happened before deciding what to do next, especially when a receipt or payment is time sensitive.

An absent statement, bank response or subsidiary forecast can leave a gap in the cash position. The account may still be accessible, but the team may be waiting for an email that normally prompts it to act.

The priority depends on our obligations and available liquidity, including whether a delayed receipt would leave enough cash for payroll or a loan repayment. I'd give the activity priority when a delay would put an important obligation at risk.

Third, I'd use the alternatives already available.

A team can continue using historical email and fax routines even when its bank offers a suitable online feature. I'd ask about those capabilities before designing a new workaround around the old process.

Some banks' platforms take document uploads, track a transaction and settle document discrepancies on screen. Some also show balances, statements, payment status and secure messages, so the information can be there even when the email alert never arrives.

The next question is our company's access. A feature listed on a bank's website is useful only when the company is enrolled and the relevant people are authorised to use it.

I'd complete that onboarding in advance, with permissions suited to the activities we have identified as critical. Where the feature suits ordinary work, using it regularly would help the team stay familiar with it.

That is a practical improvement we can make before the next outage. It may also remove an unnecessary email dependency from the normal process, rather than leave another unfamiliar tool for an emergency.

The bank needs to accept the alternative for the particular activity, and our own approval controls still apply. An informal message cannot acquire authority simply because the usual channel is unavailable.

I'd also establish that the alternative works under the outage we are planning for. Another app may still depend on the same email account, or leave the essential documents beyond reach.

A short rehearsal should expose the important gap. I'd pick one critical activity and see whether the team can complete it through the agreed route with the normal inbox unavailable.

So here's what I'd do.

I'd finish with a small set of priorities, each with an owner and an agreed alternative the relevant people have tried. Some may need a bank platform feature; others may need accessible internal approvals or a different way to obtain information.

I'd carry a pair of safeguards into any alternative, starting with verification: urgency should not make an unusual instruction easier to accept without the agreed checks.

Microsoft Threat Intelligence reported fake service notifications exploiting the November outage. The messages used the real incident reference and threatened deletion of delayed mail to encourage people to follow a recovery link.

That's a reminder to keep the established checks when a real outage makes an unfamiliar request sound plausible.

The second safeguard is recovery. A missing confirmation does not prove that an instruction failed, so I'd establish its status before sending it again when service returns.

For a smaller company or a startup, the exposure is usually one person. The bank's registered email is often a founder's, the login token sits with one colleague, and there's a single bank. I'd add a second authorised user and a second registered contact. And I'd know how payroll goes out if that person or that inbox is unavailable.

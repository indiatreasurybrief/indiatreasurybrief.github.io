Rupee forecast for next year: 96, 98 or 100? Picking your budget rate

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Rupee & FX

Rupee forecast for next year: what to put in your budget, what's pushing the rupee each way, and the one rate you can lock in.

What rupee rate should go into next year's budget? This year's plans assumed about 91 to the dollar, and the rupee was near 95 before the year began.

This episode looks at why this year's budget rate went wrong so quickly, what's pushing the rupee weaker (oil, US interest rates, foreign investors selling) and what's holding it up (the Reserve Bank, India's services earnings, the US trade deal), the one rate a treasurer can actually lock in, and three things I'd do before setting next year's number: measure this year's miss, set the hedge ratio first and let the budget rate follow, and stress test the plan at a hundred.

Back in January, when a lot of Indian companies were setting their budgets for this financial year, they had to pick a price for the dollar. Most of them picked something around ninety one or ninety two rupees. That's where the rupee was at the time, and what the forecasters expected. But by the time those budgets started in April, a dollar already cost almost ninety five rupees, so the plan was three or four per cent off before it had even begun. Now we're about to pick that number again, for next year's budget. The rupee is at about ninety five, most bank forecasts sit between ninety six and ninety eight, and some headlines say a hundred. So which one goes in the plan?

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

So today is about that one number, the dollar rate that goes into next year's budget. I want to look at why this year's went wrong so quickly, what's pushing the rupee each way from here, the one rate we can actually lock in, and what I'd do about all of it.

Start with how this year's plan rate was set, back at the beginning of twenty twenty six, because I think it explains most of what follows.

In January, the rupee averaged about ninety one to the dollar, and by the end of the month, it was at about ninety two. In February, one survey of economists put the middle of their forecasts at ninety one, so if anything, they expected the rupee to get a little stronger. A plan built on ninety one or ninety two looked perfectly sensible, and I'd have signed off on it myself.

Then the conflict in West Asia started, and oil went through the roof. Brent started twenty twenty six at about sixty one dollars a barrel, and by the end of March, it was at about a hundred and eighteen. India buys most of its oil from abroad, and it pays for that oil in dollars, so the rupee took the hit. It closed the financial year on the thirty first of March at ninety four point seven eight, down almost ten per cent over the year, and that was its worst financial year in fourteen years.

It hasn't really recovered since. In May, it touched a record low of ninety six point nine seven. Over the last quarter, from July until September, the rupee averaged ninety five point six to the dollar, and in the same three months a year earlier, it averaged eighty seven point four. Earlier this month, it slipped as far as ninety six point seven nine before the Reserve Bank stepped in, and it closed on Friday at ninety four point nine three.

So for anyone whose plan said ninety one, every dollar last quarter cost about four point six rupees more than the plan expected. At that rate, for every hundred million dollars a company pays out, that's about forty six crore rupees that wasn't in the budget. For an exporter, it's the other way round, and the extra rupees have been a welcome surprise. Either way, the rate in the plan was already out of date by the time the year began.

So where does the rupee go from here? I'm not going to give you a number, because I don't think anybody can, and this year showed that. What I can do is lay out what's pushing it each way, and then we can each weigh that up for our own business.

Start with what's pushing it weaker, and I'd say there are three big things.

The first is oil. India buys nearly nine in ten of its barrels from abroad, and between April and June alone, the oil import bill came to about sixty one billion dollars, up from about forty nine billion a year earlier. That's nearly a quarter more in a single year. Brent ended last week at about a hundred and seventeen dollars, its highest in five months, after attacks on energy sites in Saudi Arabia. So as long as oil stays this high, India needs more dollars every month to pay for the same oil.

The second is interest rates in America, which are going up, not down. The Fed raised them in September, and it meets again on Wednesday. Markets expect it to hold this time, but in their September projections, the Fed's own officials expected one more rise before the end of this year, and then rates staying at that higher level right through next year. American government bonds that run for ten years already pay about five per cent, and when dollars earn that much at home, less of that money needs to come to India.

The third is foreign investors. Between April and June, foreign investors in Indian shares and bonds took a net nine point six billion dollars out of the country, and this year so far, they've taken nearly twice as much out of Indian shares as they did in the whole of last year. Analysts count those American rates among the reasons. When they sell, the money usually leaves as dollars, and that's more pressure on the rupee. Direct investment, the kind that builds factories and buys companies, is still coming in, at about six billion dollars net in those same three months, but that's less than what the share and bond investors took out.

Now, there are real things holding the rupee up as well, and I'd say there are three of those too.

The first is the Reserve Bank itself. It has been selling dollars to steady the rupee, and earlier this month it brought back some of the tools it last used in twenty thirteen, which I went through in episode thirteen. Its reserves have fallen for six weeks in a row, but at about seven hundred and twenty one billion dollars, it's still a very large pile. It also raised interest rates in October, and in the minutes published this week, one of its rate setters said that keeping a gap between Indian interest rates and those in the big Western economies matters for attracting foreign money into Indian debt.

The second is what India earns from services, things like software, consulting and the back offices that big global companies run from here. Between April and June, India earned about fifty two billion dollars more from selling services to the world than it paid for the services it bought. A year earlier, that surplus was about forty eight billion. So that's a large and growing flow of dollars into the country, and it doesn't depend on the price of oil.

And the third is the trade deal with America. The first part of it is agreed, but it hasn't been signed, and the tariff terms aren't public yet. If it's signed on good terms, exporters will know a lot more about next year's dollars, and I'd expect that to help.

So that's three things pushing the rupee down, and three holding it up. For what it's worth, most of the bank forecasts I could find put the rupee somewhere between ninety six and ninety eight a year from now. You'll have seen a hundred in the headlines too, but none of those forecasts actually goes that far. I'd treat all of them as opinions rather than facts. We know our own dollar flows better than any bank does, so I think we're the ones best placed to judge which side matters more for our business.

Whichever way we lean on all of that, there's one rate that doesn't depend on anybody's opinion, and that's the forward rate. A forward is a price a bank agrees with us today for dollars we'll pay or receive at a set date later on. Right now, a year forward costs somewhere between three point five and four per cent more than today's rate, which works out at about ninety eight or ninety nine.

That ninety eight or ninety nine is not the bank's guess at where the rupee will be next year. It's simply today's price for dollars delivered next year. A budget rate is really a promise the business makes to itself about what a dollar will cost, and most promises like that get broken by the market, not by us. The only version we can actually keep is one we can lock in today, and the forward is the only number in this whole conversation that's actually for sale.

Now, there's a fair argument against that. An exporter that budgets at ninety nine is counting on rupees it only gets if it sells its dollars forward, and an importer that budgets at ninety nine might price itself out against a competitor who budgets at ninety five and gets lucky. I agree with that, and it's really the point. The forward is only an honest budget rate when it comes with the hedge. If we're not going to hedge, then the plan rate is our own call on everything I just went through, and I'd want a cushion on it. What I wouldn't do again is copy somebody else's forecast into the plan, because this year showed us what those are worth.

And yes, hedging has got more expensive this month. I went through why in episode thirteen, so I won't repeat it here, but it's one more reason to decide how much we'll hedge before we pick the number, not after.

So here's what I'd do, and there are three things.

First, I'd write down what this year's plan used, and how far off it turned out to be. If the plan said ninety one and the year so far is running at about ninety five, that gap, multiplied by our dollar flows, is the size of the problem in rupees, and I'd put that in front of the CFO, as a rupee figure, because that's the number that gets attention.

Second, I'd look again at how much we hedge, and let the budget rate follow from that. Most hedging policies set a share of our dollar flows that we cover, and I'd revisit that share now, rather than carry last year's ratio forward out of habit. Whatever share we hedge goes into the plan at the forward rate, because that part is locked in. The rest is our own call on what's pushing the rupee each way, with a cushion, and I'd say openly that this part is a guess. And I'd agree who signs off on all of it, because I think a budget rate with no hedge behind it is really just a hope.

Third, I'd run a stress test at a bad case rate, and a hundred is the obvious one to try. I'd look at margins, at pricing, and at any loan covenant tied to dollar debt, and I'd decide now what we'd do if it happens, rather than in the quarter it happens. And I'd agree a trigger in advance, so that if the rupee, or oil, moves past a level we've already agreed, the plan gets reopened as a matter of course rather than as a debate in the middle of a bad month.

If we're the Indian arm of a multinational, the budget rate usually comes from group treasury, so for us, the first and third of those matter more than the second.

I think the lesson from this year is simple. The number we put in the plan was never going to be right. What we can control is whether it's a number we chose for a reason, and whether we've already agreed what to do when the market moves away from it.

Later this week, IndiGo and Indian Oil report their results for the last quarter, and once those are out, I'd like to come back to what the rupee has actually done to the companies that pay out the most dollars.

Demergers in India: what a split means for treasury

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Regulation

Demergers in India: what splitting a company does to its debt, cash and banks, from a treasurer's point of view.

Fifty Indian companies have split themselves up in three years. Vedanta has missed its own completion deadline three times. And when Religare announced theirs, the share price fell ten per cent — on the announcement, not on results.

So what's changed?

This episode asks why the market has started pricing execution risk at the moment a split is announced rather than waiting to see. Why splitting a company just got procedurally cheaper, and what a shorter approval route does to your build window. And then the part that decides whether any of it works: what a demerger actually does to the debt, the cash, the exposures and the flows.

Including the questions that are far cheaper to answer before the perimeter is fixed than after it. Whether removing a business from the group trips a covenant on facilities that have nothing to do with it. Whether the smaller entity can fund itself on day one. What happens to hedge accounting when a group stops hedging centrally. And why the cash pool has to be rebuilt twice, not once.

Fifty Indian companies have split themselves up over the last three years.

The biggest of them is Vedanta. And Vedanta has now missed its own completion deadline three times. The latest target is the thirtieth of June, and there are still clearances outstanding.

Then there's Religare. When they announced their demerger, the share price fell ten per cent. Not on results. On the announcement.

So something has shifted. The market used to pay for the promise. Now it's started pricing the doubt about whether the promise can actually be delivered.

And whether it can be delivered is, mostly, a balance sheet question.

Which makes it yours.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes a day for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

So why now, and not last year?

Two reasons. The first is the calendar.

Vedanta's split took effect on the first of April. Natco Pharma has an appointed date of the first of October for its agrochemicals business. Apollo Hospitals is targeting the fourth quarter. Religare, the quarter after that. Those are dates, not intentions.

The second reason is that splitting a company just got cheaper to do.

The Ministry of Corporate Affairs widened the fast-track route in September of last year, and then again this September. And for the first time, the rules explicitly cover fast-track demergers, rather than only mergers. The threshold now reaches listed companies with dispersed shareholding. And one filing step has been removed altogether.

Now set that against a tribunal carrying something like ten thousand pending cases. For the right structure, that's the difference between joining the queue and going around it.

I should be precise here, though. Fast track doesn't cover everything. The large listed splits all still went through the tribunal, Vedanta included.

And here's the part that should worry us. If approval gets faster, the build window gets shorter. What used to give us twelve to eighteen months of warning may now give a good deal less.

And this is not a handful of companies. Siemens split off its energy business. Aditya Birla Fashion carved out Madura. Raymond listed its real estate arm. Tata Motors separated commercial vehicles from passenger vehicles. ITC demerged hotels. Hindustan Unilever spun out ice cream as Kwality Wall's. Vedanta went four ways in June. And Anant Raj, HEG and India Glycols are all mid-process right now.

Now, does any of this actually work? I want to be straight with you, because the evidence is genuinely mixed.

Studies covering a hundred and sixty-three announcements over two decades find that share prices rise when a split is announced. That's consistent, and it's well documented. But the same researchers note that improvements in actual operating performance afterwards are not consistently observed.

So the market reliably rewards the announcement. What nobody can tell you in advance is whether two functioning companies get built.

That gap is what I want to talk about. Because everything that decides it sits on a balance sheet. Four things. Let me take them in order. The debt, the cash, the exposures, and the flows.

Start with the debt, because it's the one that can stop the whole thing.

Everyone asks which facilities travel with the business and which stay behind. That's the easy half.

The harder question is whether removing assets, revenue or profit from the group triggers an event of default on what's left. And it can. Covenants are written on the group as it exists today. Take a business out, and the ratios move. We could be in breach on the day the split completes, on facilities that have nothing to do with the business being separated.

So I'd check that before the perimeter is set, not after. And by perimeter, I mean the line that decides which businesses, assets and liabilities go into the new company.

Then we should look at capacity. If the split leaves one entity holding most of the borrowing and the other holding most of the cash generation, we've created a company that can't service its debt and another that can't deploy its cash. Vedanta is carrying a heavily leveraged balance sheet, and splitting that across five entities is the single hardest part of what they're doing.

Second, the cash.

On day one, the new company's payables have started. Its receivables have not collected yet. Somebody has to fund that gap, and the size of it depends entirely on the working capital cycle of the business being carved out.

So I'd build a pro-forma cash flow for each proposed entity before the perimeter is fixed. Not a valuation. A cash flow. What does each side collect, what does each side pay, and when. If the smaller entity can't fund itself from day one, then the perimeter is wrong, and moving a line on a chart now costs almost nothing compared with arranging an emergency facility in eight months.

There's a smaller thing that catches people too. Surplus investments don't always simply transfer. Fixed deposits and mutual fund holdings often have to be liquidated and re-purchased in the new entity's name. That crystallises gains, it costs exit loads, and it almost never appears in anyone's separation budget.

Third, the exposures. There are three of them, and they behave quite differently.

Start with the hedges. The good news is that novating a derivative to the new entity does not, on its own, break hedge accounting. A change of counterparty isn't enough to force de-designation.

But here's what does. If the group hedges centrally today, and after the split each company hedges on its own, that's a change in the risk management objective. And under IFRS 9, a change in risk management objective can require you to terminate the hedging relationship altogether. So the question isn't whether the contract moves. The question is whether the strategy changes.

Then intercompany balances. Loans between the two businesses were intercompany yesterday. Tomorrow they're debt between two unrelated companies. They get settled, or novated, or documented on arm's length terms, and somebody has to decide which before the record date.

And then guarantees. There's a term for what goes wrong here, and it's a good one. Wrong pockets. It's when an asset, a liability or an obligation ends up legally held by the wrong entity after separation. In a banking context that covers guarantees, fixed and floating charges, cross-default provisions, and ancillary facilities set up at group level years ago that nobody has looked at since.

Fourth, the flows.

We'll need collection accounts across a dozen states or more, because registration for goods and services tax in India is state-wise, so the collections architecture has to be too. Bank connectivity runs to weeks rather than days.

And the cash pool has to be rebuilt twice, which is the bit people miss. The business that's leaving was a participant in the group's pooling arrangement. So the new company needs its own pool built from nothing. But the parent's pool changes as well, because one of its participants has gone, and the sweep structures and intercompany limits that were sized around it no longer fit. Two pools to redesign, not one.

But the piece that needs real judgement is the trade counterparties.

And I want to separate this from the shareholder side, because they get conflated. The registrar handles the share allotment and the shareholder records. Nobody handles the customers and suppliers except us.

So there are decisions to make. Which counterparties get an individual conversation, and which get a standard notice. Which of the largest receivables we confirm personally have been re-pointed before the record date. How we sequence it, so we're not telling three thousand vendors something that changes a week later.

Because the failure mode here isn't that people stop paying us. It's that they pay the wrong entity, for months, and nobody notices until a reconciliation.

Which is why I'd design a control rather than rely on the communication. A sweep between the two entities, set up before the record date, means misdirected receipts move on their own. I'd assume the communication will be imperfect and build for that.

And the same thing applies in reverse. Most of us are not demerging. But our customers and suppliers are. So if we sell to Tata Motors, or buy from Vedanta, it's worth checking which entity actually holds the contract now. Credit limits were sized against a company that may no longer hold the business. And the master data is probably wrong.

Now, before I close, the honest caveat.

If we've got no borrowings, no currency exposure, and a customer list that fits on one page, then none of this is urgent. It's worth knowing, but it isn't a pressing one. This bites hardest on the leveraged, multi-state, multi-bank group, because that's where every one of these four things is complicated at the same time.

So, to close.

The market pays for the announcement. That's well established. But an announcement is only a promise about focus and capital allocation, and the evidence on whether that promise gets delivered is a lot thinner.

What separates the two outcomes is whether the debt, the cash, the exposures and the flows were sorted out before the perimeter was fixed, or discovered afterwards.

Those are financing questions. Every one of them. And they're much cheaper to answer while a line on a chart can still move.

So if a split is being discussed anywhere near you, that's the moment to be in the conversation. Not because you're owed a seat. Because you're the only one who can answer the questions that decide whether it works.

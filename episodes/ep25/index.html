Inflation in India: why wholesale prices are rising twice as fast as retail

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Markets & economy · Rates

In October, inflation measured by the consumer price index, or CPI, was about 6%. The wholesale price index, or WPI, put it above 12%, and it has been at least double the CPI in every month since May. So which of the two is your company's inflation?

We cover how each index is built: the CPI's new basket from February, with food at about 37% and rent at about 11%, and the WPI's rebuild in June, with the new producer price indices beside it. Then three reasons wholesale prices are rising twice as fast as retail: fuel, what the WPI leaves out, and timing. Then what each index moves on a treasury desk. The CPI moves the price of money, through the RBI and the repo rate. The WPI moves how much money the company needs, through inventory, receivables and drawing power, and it sits in many price clauses. We close with what I'd keep track of each month, and a note for smaller companies and startups.

Inflation measured by the consumer price index, or CPI, rose to about six per cent in October. The wholesale price index, or WPI, put it at more than twelve. So which is your company's inflation? The CPI tracks prices in the shops, and it's the one the RBI targets, so it shapes what your company pays to borrow. The WPI tracks what producers charge for goods, before tax, so it's the closer guide to what your suppliers charge, and to how much cash your inventory ties up.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for India's treasury and finance teams, from big corporates to startups.

I'll cover three things. The first is how each index is built. The second is why the WPI is rising twice as fast as the CPI. And the third is what each one moves on a treasury desk, and what I'd keep track of.

So, first, how each index is built, starting with the CPI.

The CPI prices a fixed basket of what households buy, from rice and onions to rent and phone bills. Every week, the statistics ministry's field staff price that basket in nearly three thousand markets and villages, and some of those prices even come from online shops. These are the prices shoppers pay at the counter, so they include GST and the shop's margin. Each month, the ministry compares the cost of the basket with the same month a year earlier, and that change is the inflation figure in the news.

The government's latest survey of household spending decides what goes into the basket and how much each item counts. The ministry switched to it in February, replacing a basket that was more than a decade old. The CPI's base year, the year the index measures prices against, became twenty twenty four. Food and drink are about thirty seven per cent of the new basket, down from forty six per cent in the old one. Housing, water, electricity and household fuel are about eighteen per cent. Rent alone is about eleven per cent of the whole basket, and electricity and cooking fuel make up most of the rest of that group. The other forty five per cent or so is spread across transport, clothes, health, education, phones and the internet, and eating out. So food moves the new CPI less than it moved the old one.

A small item can still swing the number. Onions are less than one per cent of the basket, but in October they cost more than twice what they did a year earlier. That's why economists also watch core inflation, which leaves out food and fuel and tracks everything else, from clothes and rent to school fees and medicines. The ministry doesn't publish a core figure, so the RBI and economists work it out from the ministry's data. IDFC First Bank and Bloomberg Economics put it at about four point five per cent in October.

The RBI targets the headline figure, the full CPI with food and fuel in it, not core. The government sets that target, and in March it kept it at four per cent for another five years, until March twenty thirty one. Anything between two and six per cent is within what the RBI calls its tolerance band. October's figure was six point one per cent, just above the top of that range. The RBI's main tool for bringing inflation down is the repo rate, which feeds into what companies pay on their loans. Nothing forces a rise after one month above the band. If inflation stays above it for three quarters in a row, the law requires the RBI to write to the government with the reasons and its plan to get back. An earlier episode covered the case for and against a rise in December.

While the statistics ministry publishes the CPI, the commerce ministry publishes the WPI. It differs from the CPI in what it prices, and in the point at which it takes the price. It covers only goods, with no rent and no services. And it takes the price when the producer sells, before tax and before any trading or transport margin, so it shows what the producer charges, not what the final buyer pays. Most of the basket is manufactured goods, from steel and cement to soap and medicines.

Like the CPI, the WPI was rebuilt in twenty twenty six. In June, the commerce ministry gave it a bigger basket of goods and a newer base year, the year that ended in March twenty twenty three.

At the same time, the ministry started replacing the WPI. It launched a producer price index, or PPI, and says the switch follows what advanced economies do and what the IMF recommends. Unlike the WPI, the PPI comes as a pair. The main one, the output index, tracks what producers get for what they make. The second, still published as a trial, is an input index that tracks the prices manufacturers pay for their raw materials and other inputs. In October alone, that input index rose about two per cent, while the output index rose less than one per cent. So that month, manufacturers' costs rose faster than the prices they got. The ministry publishes only monthly levels for both so far, so these are one month's moves, not a year's.

The plan is for the PPI to take over from the WPI. Because so many contracts are tied to the WPI, the ministry will keep publishing it alongside for five years, and then stop.

So, second, why the WPI is rising twice as fast as the CPI. I'd give three reasons.

The first reason is fuel. Fuel and power are about fourteen per cent of the WPI. In the CPI, home electricity and fuel are about five per cent, and petrol and diesel for the family's vehicles add only a few per cent more. Oil rose sharply in twenty twenty six, and Brent reached nearly a hundred and seventeen dollars a barrel in late October, its highest in about four years. In October, crude oil and natural gas in the WPI cost about seventy one per cent more than a year earlier. Petroleum products cost about forty six per cent more. Fuel goes into nearly everything producers make, and the ministry lists chemicals, metals and plastics alongside fuel among the main drivers of October's rise.

At the pump, the rise was far smaller. The state oil companies raised petrol and diesel prices in May, the first rise in about four years, and by the middle of November they hadn't changed them again. In Delhi, petrol and diesel each went up by about eight per cent. Much of the tax on petrol and diesel is a fixed amount per litre, so a big rise in the oil cost underneath becomes a much smaller rise at the pump. The oil companies also held their prices while crude rose, and absorbed part of the rise themselves. So the CPI saw a much smaller fuel rise than the WPI.

The second reason is what the WPI leaves out. Rent, health care, and phone and internet bills are about twenty per cent of the CPI basket. In October, none of them rose by much more than three per cent, so they held the CPI down, and the WPI has nothing like them.

I'd say the third reason is timing. A rise in costs takes months to reach the shelf, because companies use up inventory bought at older prices and raise their own prices in steps. The figures show that delay. WPI inflation was already near ten per cent in May. CPI inflation was under four per cent then, and it climbed in every month to October.

The rises do reach the shelf. Hindustan Unilever's sales grew fifteen per cent in the July to September quarter. By its own figures, the volume it sold grew seven per cent, so roughly half the growth came from higher prices. The new producer indices are built to show this kind of pass through. The ministry says having the input index and the output index side by side shows how producers' costs pass into the prices they charge.

Oil could narrow the gap. Brent fell by about twenty per cent between late October and the middle of November, and if it stays lower, fuel will pull the WPI down.

So the CPI is a household's inflation. For a company that makes or buys goods, the WPI is closer to its costs, and in every month from May to October it was at least double the CPI.

As for where CPI inflation goes next, the RBI has given its own view. When it met in October, it projected about six per cent for the last three months of the year. For January to March, it projected five point seven per cent, still well above its four per cent target.

So, third, what each number moves on a treasury desk.

Inflation reaches a treasury in a couple of ways. The CPI moves the price of money, and the WPI moves how much money the company needs.

The price of money comes first. The RBI sets the repo rate to keep CPI inflation inside its band, and the repo feeds into much of what a company borrows. By the RBI's count, about sixty eight per cent of floating rate bank loans were linked to an external benchmark in June. That benchmark is usually the repo, and those loans reset at least every three months. Most of the rest follow the bank's MCLR, which resets less often. Bond and commercial paper yields often move sooner, because traders price the RBI's next step as soon as a CPI figure comes out. Of the CPI figures, I'd watch core most closely. A jump in onions or fuel can fade within months, but a rise in core means prices are climbing across the basket.

The amount of money is where the WPI comes in. When the goods a company buys cost more, the same inventory ties up more cash. Basic metals in the WPI cost seventeen per cent more in October than a year earlier. So a company that holds a few weeks of steel needs about seventeen per cent more cash for the same inventory. Receivables grow the same way once the company raises its own prices, because customers owe more for the same volume. Banks lend against inventory and receivables only up to their drawing power, which is a share of their value, so the company funds part of any rise from its own cash.

Fuel prices can move faster than metals. Between March and April, as oil surged, the WPI's fuel and power index rose about nineteen per cent in a single month. So a rising WPI for what you buy is an early sign that the working capital line may need to grow. Importers have one more thing to watch, because any fall in the rupee means each dollar of material costs more rupees.

Contracts are the last place the WPI reaches. A price variation clause moves a contract's price with a published index, and the WPI, or one of its parts, is often the index that clause uses. The June rebuild means old and new WPI numbers can't be compared. The commerce ministry published a linking factor of one point five three for the whole index. That means a reading of a hundred on the new WPI matches about a hundred and fifty three on the old one. But the ministry gave factors only for the whole index and its three main groups, which are farm and mining goods, fuel and power, and manufactured goods. It says finer ones wouldn't be reliable. So basic metals, which sit inside manufactured goods, have no official factor. The electrical equipment makers' association has told its members to settle how to link old and new numbers "as per mutual consultation and agreement". The WPI also stops in twenty thirty one, and the Finance Ministry has encouraged government departments to use the PPI in future contracts. So a contract that runs past then needs a clause that says what replaces the WPI.

So here's what I'd keep track of.

The first is the monthly figures. The CPI comes out on the twelfth of each month, or the next working day, and the WPI and the producer indices on the fourteenth. In the CPI, I'd read the headline against the band, then food and fuel, and then the core estimates, because core shows whether prices are rising across the basket. In the WPI, I'd skip the overall figure and go to the groups my company buys, like fuel and power, basic metals or chemicals. Then I'd read the input index as the early signal, because costs tend to show up first in the input index, then in the WPI, and last in the CPI.

The second is the RBI's own projection. In October, the RBI projected CPI inflation of about six per cent for October to December, and October's six point one was close to it. I'd hold every new CPI figure against that projection. A figure well above it is pressure toward a higher repo, and through the repo, toward higher rates on your floating rate loans.

For a smaller company or a startup, the list looks different. Since October twenty nineteen, new floating rate loans to small firms have had to be linked to an external benchmark, usually the repo. So if your working capital loan is one of them, an RBI decision reaches your interest bill within three months. A small supplier selling to a big buyer can also watch the WPI for its main input, and I'd take that figure to the buyer when asking for a higher price. A startup with cash from a funding round comes out ahead when rates rise, because the same RBI decisions set what that cash earns.

The RBI decides on the repo rate on the fourth of December, with October's figures in hand. Because the twelfth falls on a weekend, November's CPI and WPI both come out on the fourteenth of December. Between them, the CPI and the WPI tell you what your money costs and how much of it you need.

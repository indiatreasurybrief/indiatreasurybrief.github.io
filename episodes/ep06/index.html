India's rating upgrade: why your cost of capital hasn't moved

Topics: Markets & economy · Rates

India's sovereign rating upgrade: JCR raised India to A minus, so why hasn't your cost of capital moved?

On the second of September, Japan's JCR gave India its best sovereign credit grade from a major international agency in over 35 years — A minus, with the country ceiling raised too. It's real, and it's a genuinely good story for India.

But does it move the number that actually decides your India cost of capital?

This episode is about the gap between a rating headline and what's still sitting inside a treasury's own model. Moody's, S&P and Fitch — the three agencies most hurdle rates and country risk premiums are actually built on — haven't moved, and are all on stable outlook. What does a sovereign rating actually do to a company's cost of funding, in two separate channels? And where has the market itself already been pricing India, ahead of every rating agency, JCR included?

We also look at the honest counter — where a smaller agency's move can matter contractually, in a facility's rating-linked clauses, even while it changes nothing in the model.

On the second of September, a ratings agency in Tokyo gave India its best sovereign credit grade from a major international agency in over three decades.

That's a genuinely good story for India. What I want to spend this episode on is whether it's actually good news for your numbers yet.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

So, the headline first, because it's real and it's worth stating plainly.

JCR — the Japan Credit Rating Agency — moved India's foreign and local currency rating from triple B plus to A minus, with a stable outlook. It also raised India's country ceiling a notch, to an A rating. Nobody asked them to. It was an unsolicited rating, which agencies do on their own initiative when they think the market should have their view. And by their own numbering, it's the best grade a major international agency has given India since before most of us were working.

Now, here's the part I'd hold on to before getting excited.

Here's why I think that's worth ten minutes rather than a headline retweet. The number that actually sits inside most treasury models hasn't moved this week. And that's the more useful story than the one most people are writing.

Before the numbers, one thing worth knowing — Moody's, S&P and Fitch are the three agencies that actually sit inside most treasury spreadsheets. They're the ones that set a hurdle rate, or a country risk premium, or the choice between reinvesting cash in India and sending it back to head office. So here's where they stand. Moody's has India at triple B three, stable. Fitch has it at triple B minus, stable. And S&P has it at triple B, stable. S&P's own upgrade came last August — the other two haven't moved at all. None of them is reviewing.

So the honest way to put it is this. The headline changed. The number in your model almost certainly hasn't. And a single Japanese agency's unsolicited view, on its own, isn't enough to move it. That's not a knock on JCR — it's just not how the anchor works.

So let's talk about what that anchor actually does, because this is the mechanism underneath all of it.

A sovereign rating actually changes what things cost in two separate ways, and most people only think about the first one.

The first is what it costs to borrow. Every rating maps to a bit of extra interest — the market's own term for it is a default spread — added on top of a risk-free rate, to price in the extra risk of that country. And that same extra interest doesn't just apply to the government. It also feeds into what a company's own cost of capital looks like in that country. Move the rating, and in principle, both numbers move together.

The second is the ceiling. This one gets missed. A sovereign rating doesn't only describe the government — it caps how high a company domiciled in that country can be rated on its foreign currency debt. A company can very rarely be rated above its own government in dollars, no matter how strong its own balance sheet looks. That's called the country ceiling, and it's the mechanism that decides what an Indian company actually pays to borrow offshore, not the sovereign headline.

And JCR moved both. They raised the rating, and they raised the ceiling, to an A rating. Moody's, S&P and Fitch haven't moved either one. So whatever ceiling your lenders or underwriters are actually using to price your debt — and for most of the market, that's still the lower one — your real cost of dollar debt hasn't changed. The upgrade only becomes cheaper funding once the specific number a lender references has actually moved. Not when any one agency's headline says it has.

Put those two channels together and you get the actual shape of the problem. It isn't that the rating agencies disagree, which happens all the time and rarely matters much. It's that the number a treasury would use to price India — in a reinvestment decision, or in the interest rate on a bond — sits downstream of a small, specific set of agencies, and JCR just isn't one of them yet, for most lenders and most models.

Now, a number, to make that concrete.

On a triple B three country, that extra interest — the default spread — runs at one point eight seven per cent. On an A three country, it's one point zero two per cent. JCR just gave India a new A minus, and that's the grade equivalent to an A three. That's an eighty-five basis point gap, on the same country, the same government, the same cash flows. Nothing about India changed between those two numbers. Only the rating did. And right now, that gap exists only on paper, because the agencies that set most models haven't moved to close it.

One caveat on where that number comes from: most treasuries don't run this off a single public number. You weight the agencies your own way, and a lot of desks blend in their own internal credit view on top. That weighting is proprietary, and it should be — it's the desk's judgement, not mine.

But even once you've settled on a rating, turning that into an actual number — the default spread itself — is usually another proprietary step. I'm using a public source instead: a table from Professor Damodaran at NYU, a well known name in this space. It's from January, so these are recent numbers. It's not what your own model runs; it's a transparent stand-in, so the mechanism has a real number attached to it.

Eighty-five basis points isn't an abstraction either. If a lender actually priced an Indian borrower's offshore dollar debt off that better ceiling instead of the current one, that's roughly what the interest rate would tighten by. Nobody's loan documentation changed this week, because the ceiling that matters to most lenders hasn't.

Here's a second twist, and it cuts the other way.

Look at where the market already prices Indian sovereign risk directly — the credit default swap, which is what it costs to insure against India defaulting. On one recent reading, that spread sits at around zero point eight eight per cent. That's tighter than what the better A three rating would imply — and much tighter than the triple B three anchor most models still use.

So the market's own pricing has been running ahead of every rating agency for a while now, JCR included. The raters are catching up to where credit investors already are, not the other way round.

And it's worth saying plainly: a smaller agency moving first doesn't appear to be a leading signal for the bigger three. I looked at this directly, and I couldn't find real evidence that a JCR upgrade has historically gone on to predict a Moody's or an S&P move. Smaller agencies have tended to sit ahead of the majors on India for a while without that turning into a pattern the majors then follow.

I'd treat that CDS number the same way as the rating one — as one reading, not a verdict, and worth refreshing before anyone builds on it.

So, what does this actually mean for an Indian treasury.

I'd go and check two things, not one.

First, find the rating your own India country risk premium or hurdle rate is actually keyed to. Moody's, S&P, Fitch, or some blend the desk built itself. And find out when it was last refreshed. If it's triple B three or triple B minus, this week's headline hasn't touched it, and I don't think it should, on its own.

Second, if you or the group borrows offshore, find out which agency's ceiling your lenders and underwriters are actually pricing off. That's the number that sets your real cost of dollar debt, not the sovereign headline. If it's sat untouched for years, that's worth a look regardless of what happened this week — checked against where the CDS market is, not just against the rating.

Now, the honest counter to all of this, because this JCR move isn't nothing.

Some facilities carry clauses that reference "any two of four" agencies, or something close to it, for pricing or covenant purposes. An unsolicited upgrade from a recognised agency can trip one of those, even while it changes nothing analytically. I'd check the actual wording in your own agreements before deciding JCR is irrelevant to you specifically. Contractually, it may still matter, even though it changes nothing in the model.

And if your treasury doesn't run an India-specific hurdle rate at all — if capital allocation doesn't distinguish India from anywhere else you operate — then none of this changes anything for you today, and that's worth saying plainly rather than pretending otherwise.

So, to close.

Japan's rating agency gave India its best grade from a major international rater in thirty-five years, and I don't think that's wrong to notice. But the number that actually moves a reinvestment decision, or a corporate borrowing cost, comes from a narrower set of agencies, and none of them have moved yet. The gap between what they say and what JCR says is worth eighty-five basis points on a public proxy, and worth checking against where the market's already pricing it, on your own numbers.

I'd go and find out which rating is actually sitting inside yours.

New FEMA export and import rules: what your bank now decides

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Regulation · Rupee & FX

New FEMA export and import rules from 1 October 2026: what moved from the RBI to your bank, and what it opens up for exporters and importers.

The new FEMA regulations for exports and imports take effect on 1 October 2026, and most of the decisions in them now sit with your AD bank rather than the Reserve Bank.

This episode looks at what that means for a corporate treasury: realisation periods and advances for exporters, payment terms and advances for importers, netting and central treasury arrangements, and the documentation that comes with all of it.

Until now, if an exporter took an advance and couldn't ship in time, sending that money back needed the Reserve Bank's approval. From the first of October, that changes. The decision moves from the Reserve Bank to your own bank. And that isn't a one off. Almost every call in how Indian companies pay for imports and get paid for exports is moving the same way.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

On the first of October, one set of regulations replaces the old export rules and import directions, along with a hundred and sixty seven circulars that had built up around them. Most of what I've read about it treats it as a compliance reset, with new forms and new deadlines. I think that undersells it. The bigger change is who makes the call. Decisions that used to sit with the Reserve Bank, or were fixed in the rules, now sit with the authorised dealer bank we already route our trade payments through, what most of us just call the AD bank. And each of those banks has to write down, and disclose, the policy it'll use to make those calls.

That opens up better working capital on both sides of trade, and I think it finally makes group netting worth doing. There's a catch as well, and I'll come to it at the end.

Start with exporters. From October, we get fifteen months to realise export proceeds, counted from shipment for goods and from the invoice date for services. It's been a bumpy road to get here. The standard was nine months for years, it moved to fifteen last November, went back to nine in June, and now it settles at fifteen. And the bank can extend it further if it's satisfied with the reason.

If the export is invoiced in rupees, it's eighteen months, and that extra three months isn't an accident. The Reserve Bank has been building towards rupee invoicing since it set up the framework for settling trade in rupees in July twenty twenty two, and a longer window is that same long term policy turning up in the fine print. For an exporter, the practical point is simpler. Invoice in rupees and we get three more months, although the buyer is now the one carrying the currency risk, so expect them to price that into the deal.

The change I'd pay more attention to is advances. Under the old rules, if a buyer paid us in advance, we had a fixed window to ship against it, and sending the money back after that window needed the Reserve Bank. From October, there's no fixed window in the regulation at all. Shipment follows the contract. So an advance stops being something we accept nervously, and becomes something we can actually design around. If we've got buyers who'd pay part of an order upfront for better pricing or priority, the rules don't box us in on timing anymore. That's cash landing before we've shipped, and it's a real lever on DSO. The one condition is that the terms look normal for that kind of trade, since the bank still has to be satisfied that the trade is genuine.

Now let's turn to importers, because I think the change there is bigger than most people have noticed. Under the old rules, a normal import had to be paid for within six months of shipment, whatever the contract said. That cap is gone. The payment period is now whatever the underlying contract says, and the bank can extend it on a reasonable request. So if a supplier is willing to give us longer terms, and they're in line with what's normal for that trade, the regulation is no longer the thing stopping us. That gives us room to extend DPO, which we simply didn't have before. The limit on how long we take to pay is now a commercial conversation with our supplier, and not a rule.

Advance payments for imports have loosened too. Before, an advance above two hundred thousand dollars generally needed a standby letter of credit, or a guarantee from an international bank. That fixed threshold is gone. The bank now decides, based on whether the need for the advance is genuine, and it can set its own threshold if it wants one.

If we import from our own group, there's one more way to use this, and I'd think it through carefully. An advance to a group supplier moves cash to the group now. For some companies, that can do the job of repatriating cash, without waiting for a dividend cycle. But it only works with a genuine import behind it, and the regulation is clear about what happens otherwise. If the import doesn't happen within the contract period, the advance has to come back. And if it doesn't come back, or the entry in the import tracking system is left open, every future advance we make needs a standby letter of credit or a bank guarantee. So it's a real option, but it isn't a loophole.

Then there's netting. Setting off what an overseas party owes us against what we owe them now works across goods and services, which the old rules didn't allow. So a services receivable can be set off against a goods payable, with the same overseas buyer or supplier, or with their overseas group companies. It's at the bank's discretion, and it has to happen inside the realisation period.

Group netting through a central treasury arrangement or CTA isn't actually new. It's been permitted since December twenty twenty, on a net or gross basis, through one bank, under a written agreement, with both legs falling in the same calendar year. And yet I don't see many treasuries using it. In my experience, the permission was there, but the regulatory scrutiny that came with it made most companies decide it wasn't worth the trouble. Those conditions carry into the new rules. What's different is that netting now sits inside our own bank's published policy, alongside everything else the bank decides, rather than out on its own.

Why does that matter? Because if we're paying a group company and being paid by it, netting replaces a payment in each direction with a single net payment. That's fewer conversions, less to hedge, lower bank charges, and less cash sitting in transit. For a company with steady flows both ways with its group, that adds up quickly.

And this is where the shift in who decides really pays off. The bank that handles our trade now makes the call itself, under its own policy, and hence it's in a position to give us firm advice on a netting structure that actually fits our flows, and to stand behind that advice.

One more change sits right next to netting. The bank can now allow a third party to pay us, or to be paid by us, on an export or an import, if it's satisfied that the transaction is genuine. Under the old rules that was tightly restricted. In practice, it means the entity that actually pays in a buyer's group doesn't have to be the one we invoiced, which gives us more room in how we set up collections and payments in the first place.

So what does this actually mean for us? I'd treat the first of October as a reason to look again at our trade structure and terms, and not just our compliance calendar. There are four places I'd start.

First, our contracts. I'd look at which buyers could pay us in advance, to bring DSO down, and which suppliers could give us longer terms, to push DPO out.

Second, our group flows. If we're receiving from and paying to the same overseas counterparty or its group, I'd look at setting those off, and for a group with steady flows both ways, at a central treasury arrangement.

Third, our advance policy, on both sides. That includes whether an advance to our own group supplier makes sense as an alternative to repatriating cash, with a genuine import behind it.

And fourth, our bank. I'd read our bank's published policy as soon as it's out. Discretion means banks can now reach different answers on the same trade, so which bank we route through matters more than it used to. And if it'd be useful, let me know. Once those policies are out, I'm happy to read through them and come back with an episode on what they actually say.

Now the catch. The structures get better, but the documentation goes up. The rule now is that the bank can only credit or debit our account for a trade payment after it's satisfied that the transaction is genuine. At the same moment, it closes the matching entry in the Reserve Bank's tracking system for that trade. That applies to money going out for imports just as much as money coming in for exports. And because each bank writes its own policy and procedures, each bank decides for itself how much paperwork it needs before it's satisfied. Which brings it back to where we started. The call is our bank's now, and so is the paperwork standard.

It's also worth saying that discretion cuts both ways. Some banks will be cautious early on, before their policies have been tested, and companies with identical trades could get different answers. None of that makes the change cosmetic. I'd just say the benefit goes to the treasuries that prepare their case, and not to everyone automatically.

For years, the rules told treasurers what they couldn't do. From the first of October, the more useful question is what we're ready to ask our bank for.

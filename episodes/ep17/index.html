MSME 45-day payment rule: what the new law changed for SMEs

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Startups & SMEs · Regulation

Paying a micro or small supplier late costs about 18.7% a year in interest, and that interest can't be claimed against tax. This episode covers India's MSME 45-day payment rule, what the new MSME law changed on 15 October, and the MSME Form I report due on 31 October.

Almost every big company has SMEs in its supply chain, and paying them late is common: L&T's own annual report shows more than ₹3,000 crore paid to micro and small suppliers after the deadline last year. We look at what the rule says and which suppliers it covers (transporters and processors yes; medium firms, farmers and traders no), why the interest so rarely gets paid, what the new law switched on and what it left switched off, and the two rules that already apply to every private company: the tax deduction lost at 31 March and the half-yearly MSME Form I. Then what I'd do, as the big buyer and as the SME.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

Almost every big company in India has SMEs in its supply chain. If it pays the micro-and-small ones late, the law makes it pay them interest, at about eighteen point seven per cent per year once it compounds, and it can't claim that interest against tax either. I'd say that's costlier than almost anything a large company borrows. And yet paying late is common. In the financial year that ended in March, even L&T paid more than three thousand crore rupees to micro-and-small suppliers after the deadline, and it says so in its own annual report. This month, part of a new law on this came into force, and by Saturday, companies with overdue payments have to report them.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

So today is about paying SMEs on time. I'll go through what the rule says and who it covers, why it's so rarely enforced, what the new law changed this month, and what I'd do, whether we're the big company or the SME.

Start with the rule itself. It's been around for twenty years, and the new law hasn't changed it.

If we buy from a micro or small business, we have to pay by the date we agreed with it, and that date can't be more than forty five days away. If we never agreed anything in writing, the law gives us just fifteen days. And if we pay late, we owe the supplier interest at three times the Reserve Bank's bank rate, compounded every month. At today's bank rate, that works out at about eighteen point seven per cent per year, and we can't claim it against tax. I don't think there's a bank loan in the country that costs a big company anything like that.

Now, not every SME is covered, and I think this is the first thing every big company should get straight. The rule protects micro-and-small businesses, and only those registered on the government's Udyam portal. Medium sized businesses aren't covered.

The size limits went up in April twenty twenty five. A business now counts as micro if its turnover is no more than ten crore rupees, as small if it's no more than a hundred crore, and as medium if it's no more than five hundred crore. There's a separate limit on investment in plant and machinery too, and a business has to stay within both. Those turnover limits are about double what they were, so some suppliers we'd have called medium a couple of years ago now count as small, and I'd guess a lot of supplier lists haven't caught up.

So what about the suppliers most big companies deal with every day? A transport company that's registered as micro or small is covered, and so is a business that processes farm produce or runs a cold store. Farmers selling their own crops aren't covered, and neither are traders and distributors, even if they're registered. So the honest answer is that it depends on the supplier, and the only place that answer can live is in our own supplier records.

So on paper, paying an SME late is very expensive. In practice, hardly anyone pays that interest, and the annual reports show it. L&T says about twenty six crore rupees of this interest was due last year, and it paid a little over one crore. Tata Steel says about thirty seven crore was due, and it paid nothing. And it's not only the giants. Ola Electric, a much younger company that only listed in twenty twenty four, says about eleven crore of this interest has built up, and that no supplier has claimed it. To be fair to L&T, most of its late payments had been cleared by the end of March, so this is not a story about a company refusing to pay its suppliers. It's a story about how normal paying a bit late has become.

I think we all know why the interest goes unpaid. An SME that asks a big customer for interest risks losing that customer. And the other option, a complaint to the government's MSME council, is slow. The government told Parliament in February that more than half of the complaints filed over the last four years were still waiting for a decision.

When a case does finish, though, it can be expensive for the buyer. If the council rules for the supplier, it issues what's called an award, which is an order saying how much the buyer has to pay. In one case in Jammu and Kashmir, a bill of under three crore rupees turned into an award of more than eleven crore, because the interest had been compounding for years.

A couple of things also work in the supplier's favour. The complaint is heard by the council in the supplier's own state, not the buyer's. And a buyer who wants to challenge an award in court has to deposit three quarters of it first.

That brings me to the new law. Parliament changed the MSME Act in August, and the government brought most of the changes into force on the fifteenth of October.

The biggest change is for government owned companies, the central public sector companies. They now have to pay their SME suppliers through TReDS. TReDS is an online exchange, licensed by the Reserve Bank, where a supplier can sell an invoice its customer has already approved, and get the cash within days instead of waiting for the due date. The buyer still pays on the original due date, just to the bank that bought the invoice rather than the supplier. Last financial year, about three point five lakh crore rupees of invoices went through these exchanges, up from about forty thousand crore a few years earlier. So if we supply a public sector company, we should start to see our payments come through that route.

The part that would really change things hasn't been switched on yet. It gives the MSME councils fixed deadlines. It lets the government recover an award for the supplier, the same way it recovers unpaid taxes. And if a buyer appeals against an award, the court has to pay at least half of it to the supplier once the appeal has run for six months. Parliament has passed all of that, but the government hasn't set a date for it.

So for a private company, the new law changes very little for now. The deadline is the same, and so is the interest. TReDS still isn't compulsory for private companies to use, either. Bigger companies, with a turnover above two hundred and fifty crore rupees, have had to sign up to it, but nobody makes them actually pay through it.

What a private company does have to deal with today is a couple of older rules, and both are already in force.

The first is tax. If we still owe a micro or small supplier on the thirty first of March, and the payment is already late, we lose the tax deduction for that amount in that year, and we only get it back in the year we actually pay. This rule has been there for a few years, and it carried over unchanged into the new Income Tax Act this April, so next March is when it matters again. And there's a trap in it. If we never put the payment terms in writing, the limit is fifteen days, not forty five, so a bill we thought was safely inside forty five days can already be late for tax.

The second is a report to the Registrar of Companies, called MSME Form One. Any company with payments to micro or small suppliers overdue by more than forty five days has to file it twice a year, and the current one is due this Saturday, the thirty first of October. It lists what we paid on time, what we paid late, what's still outstanding, and why. So between this report and the tax return, the government can now see who we paid late, and what it has cost us.

So here's what I'd do, and I'll take the big company first.

First, I'd fix our supplier records, so that we know which suppliers are micro or small. For every supplier, I'd want to know whether it's micro, small or medium, when it registered, and what it's registered for, and I'd recheck anyone we haven't looked at since the limits changed.

Second, I'd put payment terms in writing with every micro-and-small supplier, at forty five days or less, because without that, the limit is fifteen days. And if we reject a delivery or dispute a bill, I'd say so in writing straight away, within fifteen days.

Third, I'd file this Saturday's report on time, and I'd plan now to clear anything overdue to micro-and-small suppliers before the end of March, so we don't lose the tax deduction.

And if we've already signed up to TReDS, I'd think about actually paying SME suppliers through it. It doesn't change our own deadline, but the supplier gets its money in days, and I think that matters more to them than interest they'll never ask for.

Now, if we're the SME or the startup, there are three things I'd do.

First, I'd register on Udyam before the purchase order arrives, not after. It's free, and registering late may only protect the orders that come after it.

Second, I'd put the payment terms in the contract, at forty five days or less, and I'd remember that the interest is ours to claim. Whether we claim it is a judgement about the customer, and most suppliers don't. But if a customer stops paying altogether, we can file a complaint on the MSME ministry's online portal, and if the customer is on TReDS, selling the invoice there may be quicker.

And third, if we're a startup that buys from small suppliers ourselves, the same rules apply to us. We file the same report, and we can lose the same tax deduction.

I think the honest summary is that this law is strict on paper and slow in practice. The deadline hasn't changed, the interest is high, and most of it is never claimed. What would change that is the part of the new law that's still switched off. If the government switches it on, I'll come back to it, because all that unclaimed interest in the annual reports would start to matter.

I'll see you next time.

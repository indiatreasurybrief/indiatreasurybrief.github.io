RBI rate cuts and your loan rate: why the full cut never arrived

Topics: Rates

RBI rate cuts and your loan rate: why only part of the 125 basis points of cuts reached corporate loans.

The Reserve Bank of India raised the repo rate on 7 October 2026, its first increase in nearly four years, and moved its stance to calibrated tightening.

This episode looks at how a policy rate move actually reaches an Indian corporate borrower: the benchmark a bank lends off, the spread on top of it, reset dates in facility agreements, the long road from the prime lending rate through the base rate and MCLR to the external benchmark, the Reserve Bank's 2017 study group on why transmission stalled, and the draft Interest Rates on Loans and Advances Directions now proposed to take effect in 2027.

(0:00) The cut that expired (1:40) Four benchmarks in fifteen years (4:46) What it cost us this cycle (6:54) A fifth rewrite, in draft (9:15) What I'd do about it

Through last year the Reserve Bank cut the repo rate by a hundred and twenty five basis points. Over the same stretch, the one year MCLR came down by thirty nine. And that's the rate most corporate facilities in India are actually priced off. So we got less than a third of it. The rest wasn't taken away from us. It was just waiting on reset dates that hadn't come round yet. And on Wednesday the Reserve Bank raised rates for the first time in nearly four years, so the rate that pipeline was tracking has now turned around. The cut expired before it arrived.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

So, let me take the decision first.

On Wednesday the Monetary Policy Committee raised the repo rate by a quarter of a point. That puts the repo rate at five and a half per cent. The vote to raise was unanimous, and the stance moved from neutral to what the Reserve Bank calls calibrated tightening.

It's the first increase under Governor Malhotra, and every move of his before this one was a cut.

The reason is inflation, and behind the inflation sits oil. The conflict in West Asia flared again in September, crude reached about a hundred and seven dollars a barrel, and the rupee slipped past ninety six. So the Reserve Bank has raised its inflation forecast for this financial year. It now sees five point two per cent for the year as a whole, and it expects that number to be six per cent in the quarter we're in.

The Governor's own words are plainer than the stance language, and he said three things. First, that inflation and its outlook are not as benign as they were last year. Second, that rate cuts are off the table in the near term. And third, that the next move from here is either a hike or a pause.

So, why did MCLR only move thirty nine basis points, and not a hundred and twenty five basis points? It turns on a piece of plumbing we've been through before on this show, and it's an easy one to forget, so I'll go back over it properly.

Indian banks don't lend off the repo rate. They lend off a benchmark of their own, and that benchmark has been rebuilt three times in fifteen years. Every time, it was rebuilt for the same reason. The one before it wasn't passing rate cuts on.

Let me start at the beginning. Until twenty ten, banks lent off something called the prime lending rate, and the problem with it was almost comic. Banks were allowed to lend below it, and by the end, something like seventy per cent of all bank lending was happening below it. So the benchmark wasn't really a benchmark. You couldn't even measure whether a policy cut had been passed on, because most of the lending was happening somewhere underneath the published number.

From July twenty ten that was replaced by the base rate, and the base rate was a floor. A bank published it, and it wasn't allowed to lend below it. That fixed the transparency problem, but it didn't fix the transmission problem, because a bank still worked the number out itself.

Then from April twenty sixteen, banks moved onto MCLR. That stands for the marginal cost of funds based lending rate, and the idea behind it was a good one. A bank works out what its next rupee of funding costs it, publishes that for each tenor, and prices loans off that. And it did work better. The Reserve Bank's own research compared the two regimes, and found that a one point move in policy ended up moving rates on fresh loans by somewhere between a quarter and half a point under MCLR. Under the base rate it was more like a tenth or a fifth of a point. So that was better, but it was still nothing like a full pass through.

So in twenty seventeen the Reserve Bank set up an internal study group to work out why its cuts weren't arriving. And what that group found is the part I'd want every treasurer to hear, because it's about a number in our own loan agreement.

A loan has a benchmark, and it has a spread on top of that benchmark. The benchmark is meant to move with policy. The spread is the part we negotiate, and it's the part we think we won. What the study group found was that when the benchmark came down, banks were widening the spread to offset it, fully or partly. It also found banks adjusting the benchmark itself arbitrarily. And its verdict on the whole approach was blunter than regulators usually allow themselves to be. The system of using internal benchmarks, it said, has not resulted in satisfactory monetary transmission so far.

Two years later the argument ended. In twenty nineteen the Reserve Bank cut the repo rate by a hundred and ten basis points, and banks passed on about forty. So it stopped trying to fix the bank's own benchmark and went around it instead. From October twenty nineteen, new floating rate loans had to be priced off something outside the bank altogether, like the repo rate itself or a treasury bill yield, and they had to be reset at least once a quarter. That's the external benchmark, and it's the only version of this that has really worked.

And here's the part that explains our thirty nine basis points. That mandate covered new floating rate loans to households and to small businesses. It did not cover corporate facilities. So we stayed where we were.

So what did that cost us in this cycle? We can put numbers on it.

Against a hundred and twenty five basis points of cuts, rates on fresh rupee loans came down by about seventy two basis points. Rates on loans already outstanding came down by about ninety one. And the one year MCLR came down by thirty nine. That's the one most of our facilities sit on.

In April, the Reserve Bank's own Monetary Policy Report said the cuts had reached new loans well, and I think that's fair. The easing did land. It landed on new lending, and it landed at banks whose books are already on the external benchmark.

The split between banks is wide, and it's worth knowing. Foreign banks have something like ninety four per cent of their loan books on the external benchmark. At private banks it's about eighty nine per cent, and at public sector banks it's roughly half. So how much of the easing a company actually received depended a good deal on who it borrowed from.

Now, is this asymmetric? Does a hike reach us faster than a cut does? Governor Rajan said as much back in twenty fifteen. Transmission, he said, is somewhat asymmetric, and banks tend to be a little faster raising rates than cutting them. The mechanism supports him. A bank's deposit contracts are fixed, so when the repo comes down it can't cut what it has already promised a depositor, and its own cost of money falls slowly. Loans are mostly variable, so going up is the easier direction.

But I'd be careful with the strong version of that, because I can't prove it for our kind of borrowing. I went looking for a clean measurement, cycle by cycle, of how much of a cut and how much of a hike actually reached corporate facilities on this benchmark. I couldn't find one I'd be comfortable quoting at you. What we do know is the mechanism, and the mechanism lags in both directions, because the rate only moves when the bank recalculates it and then it waits for a reset. So I wouldn't tell you the hike lands in full, and I wouldn't tell you it lands quickly.

What I would say is this. We received less than a third of the easing. The remainder was supposed to arrive at reset dates still in the future, but the rate it was tracking has now reversed. So a cut isn't something that simply turns up. We chase it, and we usually get it late and only in part. And this one ran out of time before it reached us.

Now the part I didn't expect to find when I started on this, and it changes what the episode is about.

There's a fifth rewrite already in draft. On the twelfth of August the Reserve Bank put out draft Directions on interest rates on loans and advances. It's proposed to take effect from April twenty twenty seven, and the final version will be issued separately for each kind of lender.

And the first thing to say about it is the disappointing thing. The requirement to use an external benchmark would still cover floating rate personal loans, and loans to micro, small and medium enterprises. For everybody else it stays optional. So that's fifteen years, five rewrites, and the fix has never once been written for corporate borrowers.

But two pieces of it reach us anyway, and they happen to be the two that cost us the cut.

The first is a cap on the reset. The lender picks how often it happens, it can't be less often than once a quarter, and once it's fixed it stays fixed for the life of the loan. The benchmark, the reset frequency and the reset date would all have to be written explicitly into the loan agreement. That one rule would do away with the annual reset, and the annual reset is where most of our missing eighty six basis points went.

The second is migration. Every existing loan linked to a benchmark, internal or external, would have to be mapped into the new framework by April twenty twenty nine. It needs our consent. It can't leave us worse off. The new rate can't be higher than the rate we were already paying, and the bank can't charge us for doing it.

There's an honest gap here, and I'd rather flag it than pretend it isn't there. What a loan migrates to isn't actually spelled out in anything I've read. For a corporate facility it most likely means a tidied up version of the same internal benchmark, rather than a move onto the repo rate, because corporate lending still isn't in the mandate. But that's my reading and not the Reserve Bank's words, and a treasury carrying a twenty twenty nine deadline deserves to know which it is.

The draft does two other things worth a line. It puts rules around the spread, so the credit risk part can only move if our credit profile actually changes, after a proper review, and the other parts are generally frozen for three years, although a bank is allowed to cut them to keep a customer. And the way MCLR itself gets computed would be made to track market movements more accurately, and it would have to be system generated and independently verifiable rather than set by hand. That's the Reserve Bank responding, nine years later, to the thing its own study group complained about.

So what would I actually do with this? I'd do five things.

First, I'd find the reset date on each facility. It's the reset that matters here, not the benchmark. That date decides when the quarter point arrives, and whether there's a cut still sitting in the rate that never reached us.

Second, the spread, and I'd be careful about how I looked at it. A bank can't simply widen the spread on a loan we've already drawn. Under the rules as they already stand, it can only move the credit risk part if our own credit assessment has deteriorated, and that needs a full review. Where a spread does widen is at a fresh sanction, or at a renewal. So on every working capital limit that came up for review during the cut cycle, I'd compare the margin we agreed each time. If the benchmark was falling while our margin crept up, then we've found where the cut went, and that's a renegotiation rather than a complaint.

Third, I'd ask which benchmark each facility is actually on, and I'd ask it bank by bank, because our banks clearly differ. And I'd ask whether this bank will write us a repo linked facility. The mandate doesn't cover corporate lending, so that's a question to put to them, not a right to assert.

Fourth, I'd look at the choice of bank itself. On these numbers, which bank we borrow from decides how much of any future move we actually see.

And fifth, anything we sign between now and April twenty twenty seven is what gets mapped into the new framework. So I'd put the reset frequency into the drafting conversation now, rather than inheriting whatever a mapping exercise hands us in twenty twenty nine.

And the honest other side of it comes from the banks. When the Reserve Bank proposed moving everyone onto an external benchmark back in twenty seventeen, almost every bank told it that none of the proposed benchmarks could work, because a bank's own funding cost isn't linked to any of them. Deposits are term contracts. The average cost of money doesn't move when policy moves. So a repo linked facility shifts that mismatch onto the bank, and a bank that carries it will price it into the spread. The draft cuts both ways as well. A three year freeze protects us from being repriced upward, and a bank that knows it's frozen for three years may simply open wider. And faster resets are symmetrical by construction, so a three month cap will deliver hikes just as quickly as it delivers cuts. The draft trades discretion for speed, and speed has no direction.

But the reset work pays for itself whichever way the next move goes, and that's where I'd start.

India's carbon market explained: what it means for treasury

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Regulation · Markets & economy

India's carbon market explained: how carbon credit certificates work for about 490 plants, and what treasury teams should plan for.

For about 490 of India's biggest plants, carbon has just turned into money. Beat the carbon target and you have certificates to sell; miss it and you buy them, or pay twice the market price.

India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme has set carbon targets for plants in aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, paper, oil refining, petrochemicals and textiles, and trading on the power exchanges is due to start soon. This episode looks at why it's happening now, how the certificates work, how Europe, China and America price carbon, what it could cost, where the market stands, and what treasury teams should do, inside the scheme and outside it.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

For India's biggest factories, carbon has just turned into money. The country is about to open its first real market for it. Plants that beat their carbon targets will get certificates they can sell, and plants that miss them will have to buy those certificates, or pay a penalty. I think it's a genuinely big step towards sustainability, because for the first time, every one of those plants has a financial reason to cut its carbon. And for treasury, it means that across about four hundred and ninety plants, carbon is now a cost or an income that has to be planned for, the way we already plan for oil prices and the rupee.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

So why is all of this happening now? In March, the Cabinet approved India's new climate promise to the United Nations. By twenty thirty five, the country says it'll put out forty seven per cent less carbon for every rupee the economy produces than it did in two thousand and five, and that sixty per cent of its power capacity will come from sources other than fossil fuels. A promise like that has to land somewhere, and over the last few years, it's been landing on companies, in three steps.

The first step was to measure. Since twenty twenty three, India's biggest listed companies have had to publish a core set of sustainability numbers and have them checked by an outside firm, and the first item on that list is how much carbon they put out. This financial year, that check reaches the top thousand listed companies, up from the top five hundred last year.

The second step was to make companies use more clean power. Big industrial users that make their own electricity, or buy it directly from a power plant instead of from the local electricity board, now have to take a rising share of that electricity from clean sources. It's about thirty six per cent this year, it goes up every year until it reaches about forty three per cent in twenty thirty, and missing it carries a penalty. And this one has a deadline. If a company didn't use enough clean power last year, it can still put that right, either by buying certificates that show someone else made the clean power for it, or by paying a fixed fee instead. But it only has until the thirty first of December to do it.

And the third step is to put a price on the carbon itself. Those are the carbon certificates, and they're the newest of the three. They're also the only one of the three with its own market, and that's why they matter so much for treasury.

So let's look at how the carbon certificates actually work.

The government has picked the industries that burn the most energy, and inside each of them, the biggest plants, and it's given every one of those plants its own target. The targets started with the year that began in April twenty twenty five, and they get tighter the year after. One thing that surprises people is that they aren't a cap on the total. A cement plant is free to make more cement than it did last year. What it can't do is put out more carbon for every tonne of cement than its target allows.

The first list came out in October twenty twenty five, and it covered about two hundred and eighty plants making aluminium, cement, paper and chemicals. In January, the government added oil refineries, petrochemicals and textiles, and the total now stands at about four hundred and ninety. So these are some of the biggest industrial names in the country, companies like UltraTech, Vedanta and Hindalco. And steel is very likely to be next, because a draft has been out since June that covers about two hundred and fifty steel plants.

Now, the way the money works is simpler than it sounds. If a plant beats its target, the Bureau of Energy Efficiency, which runs the scheme, gives it certificates that it can sell, and each one stands for a tonne of carbon it didn't put out. If a plant misses its target, it has to hand in certificates to cover the gap, and if it hasn't got enough of its own, it has to buy them from the plants that did better. So the plants that cut the most get paid by the plants that cut the least.

And that brings us back to the penalty. If a plant misses its target and doesn't hand in enough certificates, the Central Pollution Control Board can charge it twice the average price that certificates traded at during that year, and the company has ninety days to pay. So not buying is always the more expensive choice, and I think that's deliberate.

So that's how India's scheme works. Before we get to what it might cost, I think it's worth a quick look at the rest of the world. India isn't the first country to do this, and it helps to see how the others work. Europe has run the oldest and biggest carbon market since two thousand and five, and it works differently from ours. There, the total amount of carbon is capped, and the cap comes down every year, so permits keep getting scarcer, and this month they've been trading at around eighty five euros a tonne. Europe is also about to make its price matter to Indian exporters. From early next year, a European company that buys goods like steel or aluminium from abroad has to pay for the carbon that went into making them, at Europe's price, and that covers those goods shipped this year. Carbon costs from some countries can be knocked off that bill, but as far as I can tell, India's scheme doesn't count yet. So in time, an Indian aluminium exporter could end up paying for the same carbon twice, once here and once at Europe's border, and I'd expect its European buyers to want to talk about price.

China's market is much closer to ours. It started in twenty twenty one with power stations, it hands out permits based on carbon per tonne of output rather than a fixed total, and it has recently added steel, cement and aluminium. A permit there costs about ninety five yuan, or roughly thirteen dollars.

America has no national carbon price at all. A few states run their own markets, and in California, which runs the biggest of them, a permit sold for about thirty two dollars in August. So of the three, India has chosen a design that looks a lot more like China's than Europe's.

So how much could this actually cost?

Nobody knows the price yet, so the best we can do is borrow a sensible guess. In April, ICRA, the rating agency, looked at fourteen cement and aluminium companies, and it assumed that every tonne of carbon a plant puts out over its target would cost ten dollars. That's roughly nine hundred and fifty rupees a tonne, and it's close to China's price and nowhere near Europe's.

Even at that low price, ICRA found that some cement companies could lose as much as a fifth of their profit. I think that's because making cement puts out a lot of carbon for every rupee it earns, so even a low price bites hard. For the aluminium makers, the hit was much smaller, at about three per cent of profit.

And the bill gets bigger. The targets get tougher in the second year, so ICRA expects these companies to fall short by nearly three times as much in the second year as in the first. So even if the price stays exactly the same, the bill nearly triples.

Those are one firm's estimates, built on a price that nobody has actually paid yet, and the bigger number is for some cement companies, not all of them. But they do give us the size of the thing, and they tell us it isn't a rounding error.

For most of treasury's working life, carbon was a number in the sustainability report. For these companies, it's now a bill or a receivable, with someone on the other side, a due date, and a penalty. The only thing missing is the price. And we budget for prices we don't know all the time, like oil, the rupee and interest rates. This is one more, and the difference is that the penalty is already set at double whatever the market decides.

So where is the market itself?

The rules for trading are written. The Central Electricity Regulatory Commission, which oversees India's power markets, published them in February, and they're fairly strict. Certificates can only be bought and sold on the power exchanges, the same ones that already trade electricity, and trading will happen once a month. There won't be any private deals between companies, at least to begin with, so every certificate will change hands at a price that everyone can see. And the regulator will set a lowest and a highest price that it'll allow, although neither of those has been published yet.

What's still not settled is the start date. In March, the Power Minister said trading would begin within four months, which would have meant July, and July came and went. A month before that, a director at the Bureau of Energy Efficiency had said the first certificates would be handed out in October, which is this month, with trading some time between November and January. As far as I can find, not one certificate has changed hands yet, so the first real price could be a few weeks away, or it could be a few months.

So where does that leave these companies right now? The first year of targets has already ended. It ran from April twenty twenty five until March twenty twenty six, and every plant had to send in its results, checked by an approved auditor, by the end of July. So most of these companies already have a good idea whether they beat their target or missed it, and so whether they'll have certificates to sell or a gap to fill. What they still don't know is the price, so they can't yet put a number on what it'll cost them, or on what they'll earn.

So here's what I'd do, and I'd split it in two. Only a small group of companies are actually inside these schemes, so I'll start with them, and then come to what the rest of us should look at.

If we're one of the companies in the carbon scheme, or one of the big users that has to meet the clean power share, there are three things I'd do.

First, I'd find out which side of the carbon scheme we're on, in tonnes, for both years. The plant team or the sustainability team will have the number for the first year, and I'd ask for their best guess at the second year as well, because that's the harder target. Then I'd put a price range in the budget, labelled clearly as an assumption. I think ten dollars a tonne is a reasonable place to start, but I'd use a range, and I'd remember that the penalty is double, so the cost of not buying in time is the top of that range, twice over.

Second, I'd agree now who buys or sells, and when. Trading will be monthly, it'll only happen on the exchanges, and we'll have to register before we can take part, so the setup is worth doing before the first session rather than during it. And if we expect to have spare certificates, there's a real choice to make, because they don't expire. We can sell them, or we can keep them for next year's tougher target.

Third, I'd look at the clean power share. If we make our own electricity, or buy it directly from a power plant, I'd check last year's number now, because any shortfall has to be made up by the thirty first of December. And because the share goes up every year, I'd look at whether signing a green power contract for several years makes more sense than paying our way out year after year. I'd say that's a treasury decision as much as an energy one, because it's a long contract, and it's worth knowing exactly who's on the other side of it.

Now, there's a fair argument that all of this is very early. The first price may well be low. At ten dollars a tonne, the aluminium hit is about three per cent, and the analysts themselves describe the scheme as more of a signal than a burden for now. The market isn't even open, and its start has already slipped once. So for a lot of these companies, the first year is small money.

I agree with most of that. But I think it's exactly why the setup is worth doing now. The gap in the second year is nearly three times bigger, the penalty is double the price, and the cheapest time to agree who buys, how, and up to what price, is before anyone has started trading.

For the rest of us, which is most of the people listening, there are still two things I'd look at.

The first is the reporting. If we're in the top thousand listed companies, and especially if we're somewhere between five hundred and a thousand, this is the first year an outside firm has to check our sustainability numbers, so I'd make sure that's arranged. If we're in the carbon scheme as well, I'd want the carbon number in that report and the one we give the scheme to come from the same data. And if we supply one of the very largest companies, I wouldn't be surprised if they started asking for our numbers too.

The second is what we buy. If we buy cement, aluminium, chemicals or textiles, I'd check the price clauses in any contract that's coming up for renewal. I'd expect a supplier with a carbon bill to try to pass some of it on, and that's a much easier conversation before the first price exists than after it.

If we're not on any of these lists, and we don't buy much from heavy industry, then I'd say this is one to watch rather than one to act on this quarter.

Because for these companies, carbon is now a line in the accounts, even if it doesn't have a price yet. And when the first price does arrive, I'd rather we already knew which side of it we're on.

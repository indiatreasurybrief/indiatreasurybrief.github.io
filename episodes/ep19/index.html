Monopoly in India: why so many markets end up with two players

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Markets & economy

Monopoly and duopoly in India, explained: why so many of our markets end up with just two big players, and why the Competition Commission of India (CCI), the courts and the government are all looking at it now. Reuters reported a CCI order finding that Tata Steel, JSW, SAIL and others agreed steel prices over WhatsApp; JSW and SAIL deny it, and the case is still open. The CCI is investigating IndiGo, which carries two of every three domestic passengers, and the government has given itself the power to step in on airfares.

We cover where competition law draws the line, three reasons markets thin to two players (price wars in telecom after Jio, thin margins in airlines, network effects in UPI and food delivery), Viral Acharya's research on India's Big 5 business groups, the case for big companies, and what it means for us, including smaller companies and startups.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

This year, India started asking hard questions of its biggest companies. In January, Reuters reported that the Competition Commission had found India's biggest steelmakers, including Tata Steel, JSW and SAIL, agreeing prices over WhatsApp. JSW and SAIL deny it, and the case is still open. In February, the Commission opened a case against IndiGo, the airline that carries two of every three domestic passengers. And at the start of November, the government gave itself the power to step in on airfares. It matters, because more of what we buy in India is now sold by just a couple of companies. Most of us buy our mobile data from Jio or Airtel. Most UPI payments go through PhonePe or Google Pay, and most food orders go through Zomato or Swiggy. And almost every smartphone in India runs on Google's Android.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

This episode is a bit different from our usual ones. There's no treasury decision in it. It's about how the markets we all buy from are changing, and why.

I'll cover three things. First, where India's law draws the line. Second, why so many of our markets end up with just a couple of big companies. And third, the case for big companies, because there is one.

So, first, where the law draws the line.

India's competition law doesn't punish a company for being big. A company can win most of a market by being better or cheaper, and that's allowed. What the law punishes is how a company behaves. First, it punishes rivals who agree prices or supply with each other, which is a cartel. Second, it punishes a dominant company that abuses its position, for example by charging unfair prices or shutting rivals out.

Cartels are where the biggest penalties have come so far. The Competition Commission fined the big cement companies about six thousand three hundred crore rupees for agreeing prices and holding back supply, and the appeals tribunal upheld it.

Steel is the big case now. Here's what that January report said. A confidential order from the Commission found that Tata Steel, JSW Steel, SAIL and twenty five other companies had colluded on steel prices between twenty fifteen and twenty twenty three. It also named dozens of senior executives. The Commission's officials had read WhatsApp messages between regional steel groups. The order isn't final. The companies can still object before the Commission rules, JSW and SAIL have denied the allegations, and no penalty has been set.

And this case started with the buyers. In twenty twenty one, a contractors' association in Coimbatore went to the Madras High Court. It said steel makers were holding back supply and raising prices, and the court told the Commission to get on with its investigation.

IndiGo's case is about the second rule, the abuse of a dominant position. In early December last year, IndiGo cancelled more than two thousand five hundred flights in three days. Fares jumped across airlines, and the government capped them for a while. In February, the Commission said IndiGo looked dominant, and it ordered a full investigation into whether it had abused that position. That's an investigation, not a verdict, and the Commission said its views at this stage are only preliminary.

And in food delivery, the Commission's investigators reported in twenty twenty four that Zomato's and Swiggy's exclusive deals with some restaurants broke the law. That case was started by the restaurants' own association, and it isn't final either.

So being big is not a crime in India. Abusing it is, and so is agreeing prices with your rivals. And this year, regulators, the courts and the government are all testing where that line sits.

So, second, why do so many of our markets end up with just a couple of big companies? I think there are three reasons, and telecom shows the first one best.

In early twenty sixteen, India had more than ten mobile phone companies. Then, in September that year, Jio launched, and for its first few months the data was free. By the regulator's figures, a gigabyte of mobile data cost about two hundred and seventy rupees on average in twenty fourteen. By twenty eighteen, it cost about twelve.

The older companies couldn't match that for long. Vodafone and Idea merged, and several smaller companies shut down. Then, in twenty nineteen, the Supreme Court ruled that telecom companies owed the government far more in licence and spectrum fees than they had paid. That bill hit Vodafone Idea hardest. Now, Jio and Airtel have about three of every four mobile users. And in late October, when Jio came to the stock market, investors bid for more than twenty times the shares on offer.

So the first reason is cost. A phone network costs nearly as much to build for a few customers as for many, so the company with the most customers has the lowest cost for each one. And when a company with very deep pockets starts a price war, only the strongest can afford to stay in.

The second reason is thin margins, and airlines show it. Since nineteen ninety four, at least twenty seven airlines have shut down, merged or been bought, which is about one every year. Kingfisher stopped flying in twenty twelve, Jet Airways in twenty nineteen and Go First in twenty twenty three.

Airlines earn in rupees, but much of what they pay, like fuel and aircraft leases, follows the dollar. So when oil rises or the rupee falls, the weakest airline runs out of cash first. Even IndiGo felt it this year. From July to September, its average fare per kilometre rose by about a third, and it still reported a loss, because of fuel and the weaker rupee.

Every airline that failed handed its passengers to the ones that survived. And after Vistara merged into Air India, IndiGo and the Air India group carry about nine of every ten domestic passengers.

The third reason is the network. Some businesses get better for each customer as more customers join. A food delivery app with the most restaurants gets the most customers, and the most customers bring in more restaurants.

Payments work the same way. On UPI, PhonePe and Google Pay handle nearly eight of every ten payments. In twenty twenty, the body that runs UPI set a rule to cap any single app at thirty per cent. But it has pushed the deadline back more than once, and it now falls at the end of December.

And there's a bigger picture behind all of this. Viral Acharya, a former deputy governor of the Reserve Bank, studied India's five biggest business groups. In a paper published in twenty twenty three, he looked at their share of the assets of India's non-financial companies. It rose from about ten per cent in nineteen ninety one to nearly eighteen per cent in twenty twenty one. Over the same thirty years, the share of the next five groups fell by about half.

He also found that as the biggest groups grew, they charged more over their costs. And he suggested that could be one reason India's inflation has been hard to bring down. Not everyone agrees with him, and that brings me to the other side.

So, third, the case for big companies, and I think it deserves a fair hearing.

First, big players gave India some of the cheapest mobile data in the world. The price war that left Jio and Airtel on top is the same one that brought the price of a gigabyte down so far.

Second, new money still gets in when the prize is big enough. The Aditya Birla group launched Birla Opus in paints in twenty twenty four. Asian Paints still leads, but its share has slipped from almost sixty per cent to just over half. In flying, Akasa Air started in twenty twenty two. And in satellite internet, the government has licensed three companies, and the telecom minister said in October that India allows no monopoly.

Third, size isn't always bad for buyers. A big company can invest more, and it can take on the biggest companies in the world. Some economists have also pushed back on Acharya's paper, and argued that the fear of market power in India is overstated.

So I'd say the answer depends on the market. Where big companies compete hard, buyers win. Where they stop competing, buyers pay. And you can judge which of those you see in the markets you buy from.

So what does this mean for us? We all buy from these markets, whether it's flights, phones, steel or cement. When just a couple of companies run a market, prices can move with less pushback, and I think that's worth keeping in mind when we plan our costs.

For a smaller company, there's one more point. The law protects us too. The steel case began with a contractors' association in Coimbatore, and the food delivery case began with the restaurants' association. So if a small buyer believes its suppliers are agreeing prices, it can take that to the Competition Commission.

And there are four things to watch. First, on the sixteenth of November, the Supreme Court hears the case on airfares. Second, the steel companies are waiting for the Commission's final order, and its penalty. Third, telecom analysts expect a rise in mobile tariffs in December. And fourth, from the first of January, the new aviation rules let the government step in on fares in what they call an extraordinary situation.

I think the bigger story is that India is now asking the question out loud. For years, a market run by a couple of companies was simply how things turned out. Now the regulators, the courts and the government are all asking whether it's working for the rest of us.

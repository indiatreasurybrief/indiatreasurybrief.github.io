Where should a startup park its funding round? FDs, liquid funds or dollars abroad

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Startups & SMEs · Cash & liquidity

Where should a startup park its funding round? A round has to stay safe and within reach for a year or two of runway. When Silicon Valley Bank failed in March 2023, more than a billion dollars of Indian startups' money was stuck in it, according to India's minister of state for IT.

We cover the order that matters (safety, then access, then return) and three piles by when the money is spent; the choices in India with October's returns: current accounts and sweeps, fixed deposits, overnight, liquid and money market funds, and treasury bills; what Yes Bank and New India Co-operative Bank taught depositors; why many finance teams stop at ninety days; the RBI's new daily bulk-deposit rates; and, when the round sits with a parent abroad, dollars or rupees and the paperwork of bringing it in. Then what I'd do, including for SMEs.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

You've just raised a funding round. Where should that money sit until you spend it? It sounds like a small question, until a bank fails. In March twenty twenty three, Silicon Valley Bank in California collapsed. According to Rajeev Chandrasekhar, India's minister of state for IT, more than a billion dollars belonging to Indian startups was stuck in it. Over one weekend, founders didn't know whether they could pay salaries that week. In the end, the US government stepped in, and the money was safe. But for those few days, nobody could be sure. So where you park a round is not about earning an extra percentage point. It can decide whether you make payroll.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

This episode is for founders and finance teams at startups, and for any company holding more cash than it needs this month.

I'll cover three things. First, the order that matters when you park money. Second, the choices in India, and what each one offers. And third, what changes when your round sits with a parent company abroad.

So, first, the order that matters.

When you park a round, I'd put three things in order. The first is safety. The second is being able to reach the money when you need it. And the third, only after those, is what it earns. Most of the trouble comes from getting that order backwards, by chasing an extra percentage point and finding the money locked or lost.

A simple way to keep the order is to split the money by when you'll spend it. Think of three piles. The first pile is the next few months of salaries, rent and payments to suppliers. That belongs in the bank, where you can reach it the same day. The second pile is the rest of the year. That can go into overnight or liquid funds, which pay a little more and still come back the next working day, or into short deposits. The third pile is money you won't touch for a while. That can go into money market funds, treasury bills or longer deposits, timed to mature one after another, so some cash comes free every few months.

So a round isn't a pile of cash to grow. It's eighteen months of salaries that have to be there on the right day.

So, second, the choices in India: what each one gets you, and what you give up for it.

The simplest is the bank itself. A current account pays nothing, but the money is there the same day. Many banks offer a sweep, which moves anything above a set balance into a fixed deposit, and breaks it again when you need the money. Then there's the plain fixed deposit. In October, the big banks paid a little over six per cent per year for a one year deposit, but if you break it early, you pay a penalty.

From the first of October, a new RBI rule also helps. Banks call deposits of three crore rupees or more bulk deposits. Every bank now has to publish its rates for these on its website by ten o'clock every working day, and pay the same rate at every branch. So a finance team that checks those rates before placing a deposit, instead of rolling it over at the same bank, can earn a little extra.

Bank deposits carry one risk that people forget. The deposit insurer covers only five lakh rupees per depositor, per bank. That's fine for a family. For a startup with fifty crore rupees in one bank, it covers almost nothing.

Bank failures are rare, but they have happened here. In March twenty twenty, the RBI froze Yes Bank, and for nearly a fortnight, no depositor could take out more than fifty thousand rupees. For a company with crores in that bank, fifty thousand rupees is nothing. The bank was rescued, with SBI leading, and it has run normally since. Co-operative banks have been slower. In February twenty twenty five, the RBI stopped withdrawals at New India Co-operative Bank in Mumbai. Months later, depositors got up to five lakh rupees from the deposit insurer, and the rest had to wait.

So when a smaller bank offers about a percentage point more, I think that's the price of that risk. For most startups, I'd say one large bank is a safe home for the money that stays in the bank.

Then there are short-term funds. An overnight fund lends for a single day, mostly to banks and the government. It's about as safe as a fund gets. A liquid fund lends for up to three months, to the government, banks and large companies. With either one, you can get your money back the next working day. In the year to October, overnight funds earned about five per cent, and liquid funds about six point three per cent, close to what a big bank's deposit pays. A money market fund lends for up to a year, so it usually pays a little more again, and its value moves a little more too.

Since April twenty twenty three, gains on these funds are taxed like interest, so there's no tax advantage over a fixed deposit any more. The one difference is timing, because you pay the tax only when you take the money out.

Funds carry their own risk. In April twenty twenty, when markets froze at the start of Covid, Franklin Templeton shut six of its debt funds, holding about twenty five thousand crore rupees. Investors were repaid in stages, and in the end they got all of it back. But for about ten months, they couldn't touch any of it. None of those six was a liquid fund, but it's a reminder that a fund is not a deposit.

And there are treasury bills, which are short loans to the government. They're the safest of all, and at October's auctions, a one year bill paid a little over six per cent.

Once you've picked where the money goes, there are a couple more things to weigh. The first is how long you lock it away. There's an accounting reason many finance teams stop at ninety days. Under the accounting rules, a deposit or a bill that matures within three months of the day you buy it counts as cash on the balance sheet. A one year deposit is just as safe, but it shows up as an investment instead. So the cash line, and the runway your investors read, looks smaller. The three months count from the day you buy it, not from what's left at the year end. Liquid fund units usually don't count as cash, because the amount you'll get back isn't fixed. I'd let the date you'll spend the money decide first, and the accounting second. And I'd tell the board why.

The second thing to weigh is what rates are doing. The RBI raised its rate in October, and its minutes said cuts were off the table for now. Short deposits and funds pick up higher rates quickly. A longer deposit locks in the rate you get now, which helps if rates fall later, and hurts if they keep rising. That's your call to make, and I won't guess which way it goes.

So, third, what changes when your round sits abroad.

Many Indian startups have a parent company in the US or Singapore, while the Indian company does the work. When the parent raises a round, the money lands in the parent's bank, in dollars. That's how so much Indian startup money ended up in Silicon Valley Bank. So the question is how much should stay there, and how much should come to India.

There's a case for keeping dollars, and it comes down to the rupee. At the end of October, short-term dollar rates were a little under four per cent per year. Over the last twenty years, the rupee has lost about three point five per cent per year against the dollar, on average. If it keeps falling at that pace, a dollar balance earns about seven point five per cent per year in rupee terms. A big bank's rupee deposit pays a little over six. So on that average, keeping dollars comes out about a percentage point ahead. But it depends on the rupee falling. In a year when the rupee holds steady, you'd earn only the dollar rate. And if you lock in the exchange rate with a forward, you give up that gap, and you end up close to what a rupee deposit pays.

Either way, the costs are in rupees, so the money has to come to India in the end. I'd bring it in as you need it. But each piece you bring in is real work. Every time, the Indian company issues fresh shares to the parent. That means a valuation, approval from the board and the shareholders, and filings with the RBI on a deadline. The shares have to be issued within sixty days of the money arriving. So I'd bring it in a few large pieces, not many small ones. Some companies issue partly paid shares instead. The price is fixed once, a quarter of the money comes in at the start, and the rest is called within a year.

Whatever stays abroad, the bank matters too. Silicon Valley Bank was large, but most of its customers were tech startups and their investors. So when worry spread, they all pulled their money at once. I'd pick a large bank with a broad mix of customers. One option worth asking your bank about is a dollar account with a bank's unit in GIFT City. The money stays in dollars, but it sits in India. It isn't covered by the deposit insurer, so it's still a bank risk.

And the special dollar deposit you may have read about this summer, where banks paid higher rates for dollars, is for non-resident Indians, not for companies.

So here's what I'd do.

First, I'd split the round by when you'll spend it. The next few months stay in the bank. The rest of the year goes into overnight or liquid funds. And the rest goes into money market funds, treasury bills or deposits that mature one after another.

Second, I'd write a one page treasury policy and have the board sign it. It says where the money can go, how much can sit in any one bank or fund, the lowest credit rating you'll accept, and who can move the money. It should also say how you'll compare options: by the return for the risk you take, what's called the risk-adjusted return, not by the headline rate. Your investors' agreement may already list what's allowed, so I'd check that first.

Third, I'd keep the bank money with a large bank. And I'd treat a higher rate at a smaller bank as pay for extra risk, not as a free extra.

And fourth, if your round sits abroad, I'd bring it in a few large pieces, as you need it, and keep what's left with a large bank there.

For a smaller company, say an SME that's had a good season, the same three piles work. The deposit insurer's limit matters even more, because one bank often holds everything, so I'd make sure that bank is a large one.

Raising the money is the hard part. Keeping it safe until it's spent should be the easy part, as long as safety comes first.

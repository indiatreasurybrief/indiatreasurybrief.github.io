Advance tax dates and the money market: why liquidity swings

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Cash & liquidity · Rates

Advance tax dates and India's money market: why liquidity swings around the tax calendar, and what it means for short-term cash.

On 7 September the Reserve Bank offered to take seven lakh crore rupees off the banking system for a month. The banks took just over a third of it — while sitting on more than ten lakh crore of surplus liquidity, the most the system has ever held.

So why would a bank turn down a rate on cash it can't otherwise use?

Because of what leaves on the fifteenth. This episode is about the largest predictable liquidity event in the Indian money market, which is not a policy decision or a currency move — it's the corporate tax calendar. Advance tax on the fifteenth, GST on the twentieth, in the same fortnight, four times a year.

It asks why a drain that has flipped the system from surplus to deficit in September 2023, 2024 and 2025 still catches treasuries out. What it means that the dates are in the Income Tax Act rather than in anybody's forecast. And what three things are worth doing about it — none of which need a view on where interest rates are going.

Also the honest part: whether a record surplus simply absorbs the whole thing this quarter, and when it's not worth the bother at all.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

Two days ago, the Reserve Bank offered to take seven lakh crore rupees off the banking system for a month, through what's called a variable rate reverse repo auction.

Now, these are ordinary housekeeping. The banks have more cash than they can lend, so the central bank takes some of it off their hands for a fixed period, and pays them a rate set by auction for it.

The banks took just over a third of what was on offer.

And it wasn't because they're short of money. The system is sitting on more than ten lakh crore of surplus liquidity. That is a record.

They held back because of what leaves on the fifteenth.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes a day for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

So what leaves on the fifteenth?

Advance tax. Most large companies in India pay a slice of their annual tax bill on the fifteenth of September. Then again in December, in March, and in June.

And five days after that, on the twentieth, goods and services tax falls due.

So we've got two big payments, from most of corporate India, landing inside the same week.

Now, the money doesn't disappear. It moves from company accounts, into bank accounts, and then out to the government. And once it's sitting with the government, it's out of the banking system. It stops being cash that a bank can lend to anybody.

How much are we talking about? In a normal quarter, something like two and a half lakh crore rupees, over that one fortnight.

To put that next to the number I opened with, it's roughly a quarter of all the surplus liquidity in the system, going out over about a week.

That's the drain. And it isn't new.

In September of 2023, it pushed the banking system out of surplus and into a shortage of nearly one and a half lakh crore. That was the tightest the system had been in four years. It happened again in September 2024. Again in September 2025. And in June of this year, it took system liquidity down to its lowest point of the financial year.

That's the same two dates, and the same result, four times over.

And the banks respond to it in a fairly obvious way. They go out and borrow short-term from the market to cover the gap, which is why you tend to see a rush of certificates of deposit in the days beforehand. They're lending hard at the moment, so they want the deposits anyway.

And those CDs are being sold to somebody. Quite often they're being sold to a corporate treasury with cash to place. So this is a market we're already sitting in, whether or not we've ever thought about it that way.

Now here's the part I think is actually useful.

None of this is a forecast.

The advance tax dates are in the Income Tax Act. The goods and services tax date is in the rules. They were set before the financial year even started. Nobody has to guess at them, and nobody has to be right about anything.

And that's rarer than it sounds. Most of what sits on a treasury desk involves an estimate of some kind. A view on rates. A view on the currency. A collections forecast that's only ever roughly right. This is one of the very few things where the date is just a fact, and it's been a fact since April.

And the banks aren't guessing either. That's what that failed auction was.

The Reserve Bank asked them to lock cash away for thirty days. And they said no, mostly, because they can see the same two dates that we can, and they'd rather keep the money where they can reach it.

So what we're looking at is a central bank operation coming second to the corporate tax calendar, in public, with the numbers printed.

I'd say that tells us something about how much these dates matter. And I'd say it also tells us how little most of us do with them.

I'll admit I've rolled paper straight into that fortnight myself, more than once, without ever stopping to think about which week I was asking for money in.

So what do we actually do about it?

Three things. And none of them need a view on where interest rates are going.

First, I'd look at where our rollover dates fall.

If we fund on commercial paper, or on short bank lines, then something is maturing every few weeks. So I'd check whether any of it lands between roughly the twelfth and the twenty-second of September, December, March or June.

Because that's the tightest fortnight of the quarter. Rolling into it means asking the market for money at the exact moment that everybody else is asking too.

And moving a maturity by a week, months in advance, costs us nothing at all. Doing it on the day costs whatever the market feels like charging.

Second, if we're the ones holding cash rather than borrowing it, that same fortnight works for us instead of against us.

Banks need deposits precisely when the tax money leaves. That's when they come to the market looking. So I'd rather have our short-term deposits maturing into that window than through it. That way we're the ones with something to sell, at the moment they're the ones who need to buy.

It's the same two weeks. It just depends which side of the table we're sitting on.

And I'd not make this sound harder than it is. It doesn't mean predicting anything, or timing anything. It means that when we're placing money in July, we pick a maturity date in the middle of September rather than the end of it.

And third, the one I'd be least likely to think about. We're paying advance tax too.

Our own biggest outflow of the quarter lands on exactly the day the market is tightest. So the temptation is to fund it on the day, off a line, at the worst possible moment of the whole quarter.

I'd want that money identified and set aside a fortnight early, rather than found on the fifteenth.

So those are the three. Where our maturities fall, when our deposits come back to us, and having our own tax money ready before the day it falls due.

Now, before I close, I want to be straight about the size of this.

We're talking about basis points here, not percentage points. This is housekeeping. It's worth doing because the information is free and the dates never move, and not because it's going to transform anybody's cost of funding.

And there's a real chance that this particular quarter is the one where it barely registers.

A drain of two and a half lakh crore against a system holding more than ten lakh crore is a very different event from the same drain against a thin market. Back in 2023, the system was tight to begin with. Right now it isn't, and it's the most cash the system has ever held. So the squeeze may simply get absorbed this time. And if it does, I think that's worth knowing too.

The money also comes back. The government spends it, and within a few weeks most of it finds its way back to the banks. So anyone positioning for the drain needs to know when it unwinds as well, otherwise they've just moved the problem onto a different date.

And I'd say there's an honest cost to being clever about this. Shifting maturities around to pick up a few basis points takes somebody's time and somebody's attention. If we're a small treasury with two facilities and a current account, that time is worth more than the gain, and I'd leave the whole thing alone.

This bites hardest on the treasury that's rolling paper regularly, or running a real cash balance, or both. That's where the same fortnight comes round four times a year and actually costs something.

One last thing, and then I'll stop.

The monetary policy committee meets in the first week of October, which is just after all of this washes through. I'm not going to tell you what they're going to do, and I don't think we need a view on it either.

But it's worth knowing that the meeting sits right behind the drain. Because the state of the money market going into that meeting is partly a function of a tax calendar that we're all part of.

So, to close.

Most of what moves our cost of money belongs to somebody else - the policy rate, the currency, whatever global interest rates are doing that month.

This one doesn't. This one is a calendar, and we've had it since April.

Four dates a year, when a couple of lakh crore leaves the system, and every bank in the country can see it coming.

So we can either keep rolling into that fortnight without noticing. Or we can look at it once, move two or three dates, and then never really think about it again.

I'd do the second one.

That's it for today. Sources are all in the show notes.

I'll see you tomorrow.

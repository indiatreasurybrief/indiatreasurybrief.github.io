RBI forex measures, October 2026: the 2013 rupee playbook is back

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Rupee & FX · Regulation

RBI forex measures, October 2026: the rebooking ban, the 20% reserve on hedges and the oil companies' dollar window, and what they mean for treasury.

The Reserve Bank of India announced new foreign exchange measures on 10 October 2026: a special dollar window for Indian Oil, Hindustan Petroleum and Bharat Petroleum, a ban on rebooking cancelled rupee hedges, a lower documentation threshold, and a 20% Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve on large hedges of current account payments.

This episode sets the Reserve Bank's 2026 defence of the rupee against its 2013 playbook, and looks at what it changes for corporate treasury in India: forward contracts and cancellation, shipment delays, the one-bank undertaking, the cost of import cover, and four lessons from 2011 to 2014.

On Monday, for the first time since August twenty thirteen, the Reserve Bank started supplying dollars directly to the oil companies. In twenty thirteen, it did that a week before it opened its big window for deposits from non resident Indians. This year it opened that window back in June, it raised about four times as much as it did in twenty thirteen, and the rupee still came close to its record low last week. So the oil window isn't where this year's defence of the rupee starts. It's one of the newest steps in a defence that's run all year, and the rules that came with it are aimed squarely at corporate treasury.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

So, if you were on a treasury desk in twenty thirteen, a lot of this year will have felt familiar.

That summer, the rupee slid from about fifty four to the dollar in May, and by the end of August it had passed sixty eight. In July, the Reserve Bank gave exporters nine months to bring their dollars home, down from twelve, and it made banks keep almost all of their cash reserve with it every single day. At the end of August, it opened the oil window. A week after that, it opened a window for deposits from non resident Indians, and together with a window for bank borrowing abroad, that brought in about thirty four billion dollars. And from the end of twenty eleven, companies had been barred from rebooking a hedge they'd cancelled, with only a small allowance for exporters added the following year.

This year, every one of those tools has come back, but in a different order.

It started with the banks. In March, the Reserve Bank capped how big a position each bank could carry in the rupee at the close of every business day. In April, it stopped companies rebooking cancelled hedges, and nineteen days later it withdrew that ban.

In June, it went after dollars. On the fifth of June, the time an exporter has to bring its dollars home went back down, and fifteen months became nine. In September, it carried that nine month clock into the new trade rules, just before they took effect. What I told you on this show in September was overtaken within the week. I said the new rules would give exporters fifteen months, and six days later the Reserve Bank made it nine. What that shorter clock means for export receivables is Sunday's episode.

Also in June, it opened the window for deposits from non resident Indians, and that's the real difference from twenty thirteen. This time it came early rather than late. By the middle of September, the window had brought in about a hundred and forty three billion dollars. That's roughly four times what twenty thirteen raised. By early October, the reserves were about fifty one billion below their September record, although some of that is just valuation.

So by October, the biggest tool from twenty thirteen had already been used, at four times the size, and the rupee was still close to its record low. Then the rest of the old kit came out in a single week. Last Friday, banks were told that from this Friday they'll have to keep almost all of their cash reserve with the Reserve Bank every day, and the last time it asked for that was July twenty thirteen. And on Saturday came the oil window, along with a new set of rules on how companies hedge.

So, what does all of that change for us?

The oil window is the biggest single change to the market. Since Monday, the Reserve Bank has been meeting the entire daily dollar needs of Indian Oil, Hindustan Petroleum and Bharat Petroleum, until further notice. One estimate puts their demand somewhere between ten and twelve billion dollars a month, and none of that now has to go through the market. And unlike twenty thirteen's swap, this time it's a sale, so those dollars leave the reserves for good.

For us, the window does three things. It takes the biggest buyer out of the market, which supports the rupee for now, and that helps anyone buying dollars and hurts anyone selling them. It also drains rupees, because every dollar the Reserve Bank sells takes rupees out of the banking system, and I think that's part of why short term rates and the forward premium have been rising. And it's temporary, so the support goes when the window does.

The hedging rules are the part that lands on us, and they change habits most desks have had for years, so I'll take them slowly.

Let me start with what a forward is. Say we owe a supplier a million dollars in three months. Today, we agree with our bank to buy that million in three months at ninety seven. Whatever the rupee does in between, we pay ninety seven. That's the hedge, and the numbers are only for illustration.

Cancelling means ending that forward early and settling the difference in cash. If the rupee weakens and the three month rate reaches a hundred, our contract at ninety seven is worth three rupees on every dollar, and on a million dollars that's thirty lakh. If we cancel, the bank pays us that, and we're unhedged.

And here's the habit. A desk that thinks the rupee will bounce back cancels, keeps the thirty lakh, and waits. If the rupee comes back, it books a fresh forward at ninety seven, and with the thirty lakh in hand, it's effectively paying ninety four. If the rupee keeps falling and it has to book again at a hundred and one, it's paying ninety eight, which is worse than the ninety seven it gave up. So cancelling and rebooking turns a hedge into a bet. Exporters do the mirror image, and that's the version a central bank worries about in a falling currency, because an exporter who cancels a forward sale is taking dollars out of the market at exactly the wrong moment. Fewer dollars coming in weakens the rupee further, and a weaker rupee tends to push more money out, so the fall feeds on itself.

Since last Saturday, we can still cancel, but no bank is allowed to let us rebook that contract. And I think banks will read that as covering the underlying payment, not just the contract. So cancelling now means staying unhedged on that payment.

We'll feel this first when a shipment runs late, and that happens all the time. Say we've hedged for the fifteenth of January, and the supplier now ships for the fifteenth of March. Before Saturday, we'd cancel the January forward the day we heard, and book a new one for March. Now, we'd keep the January contract until it matures, and roll it forward on that date, because rolling over at maturity is still allowed. What's gone is the ability to fix it early.

There are three more rules in the package. Every hedge now comes with a written undertaking from us that the same payment isn't hedged with another bank, and if we've split it across banks, the undertaking lists what's booked elsewhere. Hedging a payment once was always the rule. What's new is that a double hedge is now our own false declaration, rather than a gap in the bank's checks.

The amount we can hedge without showing the bank the underlying trade has come down as well. It used to be a hundred million dollars across all our banks, and it's now five million.

And the last rule is a new reserve. When a company buys foreign currency against rupees to hedge a current account payment, which mostly means imports, and the contract is above two million dollars, the bank now has to keep a fifth of it in cash at the Reserve Bank until the contract ends. Banks have asked whether that includes interest on foreign loans. The circular doesn't say whether that cash earns anything. Unless it earns a full market rate, it's a cost to the bank, and I'd expect most of that cost to be passed on to us in what we pay for cover. Bankers speaking to Reuters put the extra somewhere between eighty and a hundred and fifty basis points, and some are already choosier about which importers they'll quote, with larger companies first in line.

According to clearing house figures reported by Reuters, companies bought a record seventy seven billion dollars forward in September, against forty one billion of forward sales. That matters because a bank that sells us dollars forward usually buys dollars to cover itself, so when importers all hedge at once, that buying lands on the rupee today. So the reserve is aimed squarely at importers' hedging, and that means it's aimed at us.

On Monday, the Reserve Bank went further again. It extended the same reserve to the banks' own hedges, with no minimum size, and it said offshore contracts for non residents could only be used for hedging. That's a cost to us as well, just a less direct one, because a bank that pays more to hedge its own dollar book will look to recover it in what it charges us for dollar loans and forward cover.

So how have the last couple of days gone? The rupee had its best day since April on Monday, on a day when crude also fell, so I wouldn't put that down to the measures alone, and it's kept firming since. But the cost of forward cover went up, not down. The three month premium climbed by about thirty basis points, and at about five point seven per cent it's at its highest since April. Part of that is importers using the bounce to hedge, and part of it is rupee liquidity being tightened. And the questions the banks have sent the Reserve Bank, on how the reserve works in practice, are still waiting on an answer.

So for an importer, cover now costs more twice over, even with the rupee stronger.

For years, a hedge in India was something the desk could manage after booking it. We could change our minds, move a date, and try again. From this week, it's a decision we make once. The thinking that used to happen on the dealing screen, after the trade, now has to happen before it.

So, what can we take from twenty thirteen? I think there are four lessons, and each one changes something I'd do this week.

The first is about how long rules like this last. In April, the rebooking ban lasted nineteen days. The last one came in at the end of twenty eleven, it was only eased in stages, and it wasn't properly lifted until January twenty fourteen, twenty five months after it started. So we have one precedent of nineteen days and one of twenty five months, and I wouldn't bet a hedge on the short one. I'd treat every cancellation as permanent, and I'd want none of them done without the treasurer signing off. Any hedge booked before last Saturday also carries none of the new reserve cost, which is another reason not to give one up lightly.

The second is that cover was dearest exactly when the defence was strongest. In July twenty thirteen, when the Reserve Bank squeezed rupee liquidity, forward premiums jumped across every tenor, by between eighty and a hundred and sixty basis points according to reports at the time. This week's rise in the premium looks like the same pattern. So the cheap time to make hedging decisions is before the squeeze, not in the middle of it. That's why I'd write our rules down now, while there's time to think: what we hedge, how far out, and who signs off. And if we import, I'd ask each bank in writing how it's pricing the new reserve, and whether a contract we roll over attracts it, because the cost of the reserve grows with how long the contract runs.

The third is that the oil window doesn't last for ever. In twenty thirteen, it was open for about three months. By early December, the oil companies were back buying their dollars in the market. So some of the calm in the market right now is borrowed. When this window closes, something like ten billion dollars a month of demand comes back, and I'd treat that announcement as one worth watching for.

And the fourth is that the turn in twenty thirteen had three causes, not one. The rupee did come back within about three weeks of the oil window opening. But it came back on the oil window, the deposit window, and a decision by the Federal Reserve that nobody in Mumbai controlled. So I wouldn't plan around this working, or around it failing. In episode five, I described the Reserve Bank as smoothing the rupee's fall. This year looks a lot more like holding it, and whether that works, I genuinely don't know.

There are a couple more things I'd do this week. I'd check whether the hedges we booked without paperwork add up to more than five million dollars across our banks, because if they do, I'd expect the banks to ask for documents. And I'd keep one register of which payment is hedged with which bank, because that's what makes each signed undertaking true.

If we export, the reserve doesn't touch us, but the rebooking rule does, and so does the nine month clock. If our import hedges are genuinely two million dollars or under, the reserve doesn't apply either, although I wouldn't go splitting contracts to get there. And for a disciplined desk that already books once and holds to maturity, the real change is the cost of import cover, and the lost room when a shipment slips.

So the lesson from twenty thirteen isn't about where the rupee goes. It's about how to run a desk while flexibility is being rationed, with export dollars that come home sooner, import cover that costs more, and hedges that can't be undone.

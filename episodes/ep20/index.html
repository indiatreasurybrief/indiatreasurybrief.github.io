AI in treasury: how it helps, where we are, and what's next

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Tech & operations

AI in corporate treasury: how it's helping Indian treasury teams today, where most of us really are, and what comes next. Ask the finance chiefs of big companies whether they use AI, and 93% say yes. Ask senior treasury leaders whether it's working in their treasury, and 3% do.

We take it in three steps. Efficiency: AI for bank reconciliation and payment matching, with Microsoft, Hindustan Unilever and Shell. Decisions: AI cash forecasting and FX hedging, wanted most and proven least. Action: agentic AI, meaning AI agents that move money on their own, from a BIS test of ChatGPT as a cash manager to Alphabet's treasury agents, and where the FSB, NPCI and the RBI draw the line. Then what I'd do, including for startups and SMEs.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

If you ask the finance chiefs of big companies whether they use AI, nearly all of them say yes. In one survey this summer, ninety three per cent did. But if you ask treasurers whether AI is doing real work in their treasury, the answer collapses. In one survey of senior treasury leaders, just three per cent said they'd made it work. Both groups are telling the truth, because they're answering different questions. And the gap between their answers shows where treasury really stands on AI. So in this episode, we'll look at what AI is doing in treasury now, what it's starting to decide, and how far we should let it go on its own.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

This one runs a bit longer than usual.

So let's start with those numbers.

The first number comes from Deloitte, which surveyed the finance chiefs of two hundred large North American companies, and ninety three per cent said their company uses AI across its key operations. The second comes from Citi and a couple of partners, which surveyed seventy five senior treasury leaders last year, and just three per cent said they'd put generative AI to work successfully in their treasury.

The difference is in the question. If the finance team uses an assistant to draft emails, a finance chief can fairly say the company uses AI. The treasurers were asked something harder: whether AI is doing part of the treasury's work. For most of us, it isn't yet.

Here in India, most treasuries start further back. Last year, EY asked eighty five Indian treasurers about the tools they use, and nearly one in three said they don't use a formal treasury system at all. And on AI, more than four in ten had no clear use for it yet.

I'd think about AI in treasury as three steps. The first is efficiency, which means AI doing the routine work faster and more accurately. The second is decisions, which means AI helping with calls like the cash forecast and the hedge. The third is action, which means AI moving money on its own, without a person signing first. Most of us are still on step one.

So, step one is efficiency.

This is where AI is really being used, and where most of the hard results are. When Coalition Greenwich asked big companies where AI helps treasury most, the top answer was reconciliation, which is matching bank statements with our books. More than half already use AI for it, or plan to within a year.

Microsoft is a good example. It uses AI for cash application, which means working out which invoices each customer payment is for. The AI matches each payment to its invoices, using customer emails and other data. By Microsoft's own count, it used to match about forty per cent of payments correctly. Now it's about ninety per cent, and the team spends its time on the exceptions.

Here in India, Hindustan Unilever's treasury shows what step one looks like. In an award entry published in early twenty twenty four, the team described linking its bank accounts directly to its systems, pulling every balance with one click, and sending investment instructions through an approval workflow. It put the saving at about twelve hundred hours per year. But only one of those tools was described as AI. That's typical. A lot of what gets called AI in treasury is plain automation, and it's still worth doing.

However, not every first attempt works. At Shell, a treasury manager built an AI assistant to answer the team's policy questions, like how to issue a bank guarantee. The first version made things up, and people soon stopped using it. It came back only after the team moved it into Microsoft Teams, where people already work, and gave it far more material to draw on.

So step one is real, and it's paying off. It's the plumbing, so it's the least exciting part of the story. But that's why it works, because every answer the AI gives here can be checked.

So, step two is decisions, and the obvious place to start is the cash forecast.

Every survey I looked at puts the cash forecast at or near the top of treasury's wish list. It's also the job treasurers most want AI to do. But wanting it is not the same as doing it. In July, EuroFinance, which runs the big treasury conferences, asked treasurers about AI forecasting, and nearly half knew about it but had no plans to use it yet.

And I couldn't find any bank, consultancy or treasury body that has measured whether AI makes forecasts more accurate across companies. There are plenty of claims. EY in India, for example, says forecast accuracy could reach ninety per cent, but that's an estimate, not a published result. And when one peer group asked its treasurers, the gain they reported most often was time saved, not a more accurate forecast.

Some companies are getting real use from it. AstraZeneca's treasury built a model that predicts when customers will actually pay, not when the invoice says they should, and that lets it see about five weeks ahead instead of one. But people in treasury still make the funding and investing decisions. The model just gives them a better view.

On hedging, the split is even clearer: AI advises, and people decide. Microsoft's treasurer, Jayna Bundy, has described an AI agent that reads the news and spots risks to the company's currency exposures, in Argentina for example. It then recommends what to do, so the team doesn't have to meet every time the market moves. But in Bundy's words, treasurers will still make the ultimate decisions. Agilent, one of America's big scientific instrument makers, is even more careful. Its assistant treasurer at the time said: "There's human judgment involved in deciding how much to hedge." And Agilent kept the actual trading manual, on purpose, as a control.

The other thing that keeps coming up is the data. The treasurer of one of Hollywood's biggest talent agencies still runs an Excel forecast alongside the AI, and treats the AI as a second opinion. That treasurer also gave the best example I've seen of why data matters. If one big acquisition payment is wrongly labelled as payroll, the model learns to expect a giant payroll every May. And Agilent's former assistant treasurer says a useful AI forecast needs between twelve and twenty four months of clean history, and if nobody can explain what the forecast says, the team goes back to its spreadsheets.

I also looked for an Indian company that says on the record that AI makes its treasury decisions, and I couldn't find one. That doesn't mean nobody's trying. I think it means the proof is still mostly sales material, so the honest way in is to test it on our own numbers.

So, step three is action, which means AI acting on its own.

The best test of this came out late last year. Researchers at the Bank for International Settlements, the central banks' own bank, gave ChatGPT a cash manager's job in a simulated payment system. In one test, it had ten dollars, a couple of small payments of a dollar each waiting to go out, and an urgent payment of ten dollars that might arrive later in the day. It held the small payments back, so the full ten dollars would be there if the urgent one came. That's exactly what a careful cash manager would do. It also did something I liked. When a client suddenly asked it to send fifty million dollars, far more than usual, it checked with a person before making the payment.

But the researchers ran each test many times. On the simplest version, it gave the same careful answer every time, but on the hardest, it gave the same answer only eight times out of ten. For a chatbot, that's impressive. For a payment system, it's a problem, because the same inputs must give the same decision every time.

In real treasuries, I couldn't find a single large company that says on the record it lets an AI agent pay, sweep cash, invest or book a hedge without a person signing first. Microsoft is typical of the big treasuries. Its agent can also suggest moving cash between accounts to avoid an overdraft charge. But Microsoft's treasury director said this year that, for now, people still carry out the transactions, and the agents only help them decide.

The furthest example I've seen is Alphabet, Google's parent. In September, its treasury won the prize for best use of AI in treasury at the Adam Smith Awards for Asia. The judges said its agents forecast surplus cash, review the rates on offer, invest the cash and write the accounting entries, and they called it a new best practice. But the full case study isn't out yet, so I can't tell you where a person signs off. I'd want to know that before calling it fully automatic.

Some of what gets called autonomous treasury isn't AI at all. Siemens tops up its local bank accounts automatically from its central pool, but that runs on fixed rules, like any cash pool. And one of the first real business payments made by an AI agent was only a pilot. HSBC and Mastercard ran it in Singapore at the end of May, on card rails, not a bank transfer.

Now, the people writing the rules have got there before most treasuries. In October, the Financial Stability Board, which brings together the regulators of the biggest economies, published its final practices for AI in finance. Its section on AI agents is very specific. It isn't binding, but it says a person should approve any transaction above a set value, and anything that can't be undone. It wants a tested kill switch for every agent, and one named person accountable for each. It doesn't set the amounts, though. And in one of the paper's own examples, a bank has banned its AI agent from moving any money at all, at least for now.

But not every regulator thinks a person can check everything. In June, Sarah Breeden, a deputy governor at the Bank of England, said that having a human approve every action an agent takes probably isn't realistic, and the Bank is looking at kill switches that could work across the whole market.

Here in India, the same line was drawn at the Global Fintech Fest in September. The chairman of the body that runs UPI said AI may recommend, but settling a payment has to follow fixed rules that can be audited. And Rohit Jain, a deputy governor of the Reserve Bank, put it in one line: "An institution may outsource the computation, but it cannot outsource the consequence."

The Reserve Bank is also writing this into rules for banks. Its draft on model risk, from June, says a bank must keep a list of every model it uses, have each one tested by someone other than the person who built it, and be able to override its AI models, even when a vendor built them. It isn't final yet, and it's written for banks, not for us. But I'd use it as a checklist for our own treasury, and I went through it in detail in episode fourteen.

As far as I can tell, the treasuries that have gone furthest have all drawn the same line, whether anyone told them to or not. AI does the work we can check afterwards, like matching a payment to an invoice. It gives a second opinion on the calls we make, like the forecast. But the moment money leaves the account, a person signs. That isn't timidity. It's Rohit Jain's line: we can hand over the working, but not the consequence.

So here's what I'd do, and it follows the three steps.

First, I'd pick one job we can check, like reconciliation or matching payments to invoices. Before trying anything, I'd write down the current match rate and how many hours the job takes, because without that, we can't tell whether the AI helped. And I'd fix the process first. Microsoft's treasury director, Kathy Brustad, put it in five words: "Don't automate a broken process."

Second, I'd run an AI forecast alongside our own for a quarter, without acting on it, and compare them every month. Nobody has published a forecasting gain we can borrow, so the only proof that counts is our own numbers.

Third, I'd write our line for step three on a single page now, before a bank or a vendor draws it for us. I'd use the Financial Stability Board's headings. What may an agent do, like moving cash between our own accounts, and up to what amount? What may it never do, like paying someone new or booking a hedge? And who approves anything above the limit, and who can switch it off? Then I'd ask each of our banks, and our treasury system vendor, one question: what do your AI features already do without someone clicking approve?

Now, there's a fair argument against all this. The people selling AI are writing most of this story, and the big numbers come from vendors and award entries. When Boston Consulting Group asked finance chiefs last year, the typical return on AI was about half what they'd aimed for. In India, Grant Thornton found that only eight per cent of finance teams see significant measurable value from it. So, the argument goes, we should spend the money on connecting our bank feeds and leave AI alone for now.

I agree with most of that. It's why I'd start with a boring job we can check, and treat step two as a test, not a purchase. But even if we buy nothing, our banks are building AI into the services we already use. DBS already has an AI assistant serving three hundred and fifty thousand business customers in Singapore, and HSBC has started letting business customers in Asia connect their own AI tools to their bank data. So writing our line on a single page now costs almost nothing, and it protects us either way.

If we're a smaller company or a startup, the order changes. If we still copy balances from each bank's portal into a spreadsheet, the first job isn't AI at all. It's getting our data into one place, because AI on messy data just makes mistakes faster. And the first AI we meet will probably be built into our accounting software or our bank's app. So I'd ask those vendors the same question: what does it do without someone clicking approve?

In the end, the line that matters isn't about technology. AI already does a lot of the work, and it'll do more. But when money leaves the account, I'd want a person to have signed for it, and I'd want us to have written down why.

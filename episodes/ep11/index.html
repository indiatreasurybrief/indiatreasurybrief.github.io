Rupee, rates and oil: the July to September quarter in review

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Rupee & FX · Rates · Markets & economy

Rupee, rates and oil, July to September 2026: oil rose about 40% and the rupee fell only about 1.4%, so where did the pressure go?

A special quarter-end edition. Between July and September, Brent crude rose by about 40% and the rupee fell only about 1.4%. This episode looks at where that pressure went: how the Reserve Bank held the rupee with its swap windows, record reserves and a forward book that nearly doubled; what happened to foreign direct and portfolio investment; why three-month money got cheaper while everything from six months out got more expensive; how June's rupee forecasts turned out; and the four signals and four dates worth watching between now and December.

On the first of July, a barrel of Brent crude cost about seventy two dollars. On Monday it touched a hundred and eight, and it's still around a hundred today, so oil is up by about forty per cent in three months. For a country that imports most of its oil, you'd expect the rupee to have had a terrible quarter. It hasn't. It's fallen by about one and a half per cent. So where did all that pressure go? I think it went into our borrowing rates, and that's what I want to look at as the quarter closes.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

This is a special edition. September ends today, and with it, one of the more testing three months that India's treasurers have had in some time. Rather than one story, I want to look back at the whole period. What happened to the rupee, where the pressure went instead, how the forecasters did, and what's worth watching between now and December. The exact figures, with their dates, are in the show notes.

Start with the rupee. At the end of June it was at about ninety four sixty to the dollar. Today it's right around ninety six. The record low, from the twentieth of May, was just under ninety seven, and that's still standing.

That's a very small move for a quarter like this one, and it didn't happen by itself. In early June, the Reserve Bank opened what it called swap windows. It gave banks a cheap way to bring in dollars, mostly through deposits from non residents, locked in for three to five years. By the middle of September, those windows had brought in a hundred and forty three billion dollars. It worked so well that the Reserve Bank shut the deposit window a month early. That money took India's foreign exchange reserves from about six hundred and sixty seven billion dollars in June to a record of about seven hundred and eighty six billion in early September.

But those dollars aren't free. The Reserve Bank has promised to hand them back when the swaps mature. You can see that promise in its forward book, which is the dollars it has agreed to deliver in the future. At the end of June that book stood at about a hundred and three billion dollars. By the end of August, it was two hundred billion. So the calm in the rupee this quarter was paid for with money that falls due from twenty twenty nine onwards.

And the Reserve Bank hasn't stepped back. Currency dealers say that this week, state run banks have been selling dollars on its behalf, to stop the rupee going past ninety six. Reserves fell by almost fifteen billion dollars in the latest week of data.

Then there's foreign money, and it comes in two kinds. Foreign direct investment, or FDI, is companies from abroad putting money into businesses here. Foreign portfolio investment, or FPI, is funds buying our shares and bonds.

For the first half of this year, FPI was leaving. Between January and June, about twenty three billion dollars went out, and in the April to June quarter, India's overall balance of payments was in deficit by about eight billion dollars.

This quarter was different, because of FDI. In July alone, net FDI was over seven billion dollars. That's the highest in five years, and close to what came in over the whole of last financial year.

FPI, on the other hand, just made a round trip. About seven billion dollars came in over July and August, and about six billion went back out in September, as oil climbed and American rates went up. So over the quarter, it roughly broke even.

That's why I wouldn't read the rupee's quarter as strength. FDI helped, FPI didn't, and what really held the line was a central bank standing in the way.

So if the pressure didn't go into the rupee, where did it go? I think it went into rupee interest rates, and the shape of that move is the most useful thing in this review.

The repo rate didn't change all quarter. It's still five point two five per cent. But look at the rest of the market.

Three month money actually got cheaper. A three month certificate of deposit, which is what a bank pays to borrow from investors like us, fell from about six point four four per cent at the end of June to about six point oh six. That's nearly four tenths of a point lower.

But once we look further out, from about six months onwards, borrowing got more expensive. A one year treasury bill now costs about six per cent, against five and a half in June, so that's half a point more. The one year swap rate, which is the market's cleanest bet on where the repo rate is heading, is up by about a third of a point. And the ten year government bond, what the market calls the ten year G-sec, went from about six and three quarters to about seven point one five.

So why would short money get cheaper while everything longer gets dearer? Because two different things are pulling on the two ends.

The short end is cheap because the banking system is flooded with cash. A lot of those dollar deposits were turned into rupees, and in early September the surplus in the system passed ten lakh crore. When there's that much spare cash about, nobody has to pay up to borrow for three months.

The longer end is pricing rate hikes. Oil feeds inflation. The American Federal Reserve raised its rate in the middle of September. And now banks are saying it out loud. The chief economist at SBI, Soumya Kanti Ghosh, has said he strongly advocates a quarter point hike at the October meeting, and another in December. HSBC expects two quarter point hikes this financial year, which would take the repo rate to five point seven five.

And here's the part I'd watch most closely. The cheap short end is being taken away on purpose. In September, the Reserve Bank sold one lakh crore from its G-sec holdings to pull cash out of the system, in three rounds, with the last one on Monday. The surplus has already roughly halved. HSBC expects it to take out another five to six lakh crore over time. The governor has also said the aim is to bring overnight rates back up to the repo rate, and that matters if a hike is going to work at all.

So the rupee's quiet quarter wasn't the absence of pressure. It was the Reserve Bank standing in front of it with borrowed dollars, and the pressure found its way out through rates instead.

For a treasury, I think the practical point is the six month line. If our next borrowing is short, commercial paper or a short loan, it's cheap today, but I wouldn't assume it stays this cheap into the hike meetings, because the cash that's making it cheap is being drained. And if we're placing surplus, anything beyond six months already pays for the hikes the market expects. So it's worth knowing, for each piece of funding or investment coming up, which side of that line it sits on.

Now to the forecasters, because this is a quarter where we can actually check their work.

Right at the start of the quarter, Reuters polled forty four currency strategists. Their median for three months out, which is today, was ninety four and a half. A Business Standard poll in early June put the end of September at about ninety four point nine. So the consensus expected the rupee to be roughly where it started, and it's about a rupee and a half weaker.

Goldman Sachs was closer. In June it said ninety six in three months, and ninety seven in a year. But it's the reasoning I find most interesting. Goldman expected the new inflows to limit the fall, and it was right about that. It also expected them to be used to rebuild reserves and to unwind the Reserve Bank's forward book. The reserves part happened. The forward book nearly doubled instead.

And there's a pattern worth knowing about. When Reuters polled again in early August, the rupee was at about ninety five and a quarter, and the three month median came out almost exactly there. Other banks' published numbers for the end of this year sit roughly between ninety five and a half and ninety eight. The median tends to stay close to wherever the rupee already is, and the real disagreement between them is almost entirely about oil. So I'd use them as a range, not as a number.

So what's worth watching? I'd keep it to four signals, and they're all free.

The first is Brent. Oil fell this week because Middle East exports picked up again, and talks on reopening the Strait of Hormuz are still going. A deal could take oil down fast. It happened in June. And no deal could take it back up.

The second is foreign portfolio flows, which the depositories publish every day.

The third is the Reserve Bank's reserves, every Friday evening. The weekly change now tells us how much it's spending to hold the rupee.

And the fourth is the one year swap rate, which the benchmark administrator publishes daily. If it keeps rising, the market's pricing more hikes.

The forward book comes out once a month. It's the slow one, but it's where the borrowed dollars show up.

Then there are four dates to put in the diary. The Reserve Bank's rate decision on the seventh of October. The Federal Reserve on the twenty eighth of October. Then the Reserve Bank again on the fourth of December, and the Federal Reserve on the ninth.

And one line for state owned companies only. The Reserve Bank's swap facility for external commercial borrowings, which fixes the hedging cost at one and a half per cent a year when the market charges a bit over three, is open for drawdowns until the thirty first of December.

Oil up by forty per cent in a quarter would usually be a rupee story. This time the currency barely moved, and the bill turned up in a different place, in our borrowing rates, and in a forward book the Reserve Bank will be paying down for years. So I think the question for the next quarter isn't really where the rupee goes. It's how much longer the Reserve Bank keeps standing in front of it, and what that does to the rates we borrow at.

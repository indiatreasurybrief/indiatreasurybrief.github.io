GIFT City treasury centres: is it at a tipping point?

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Regulation · Rupee & FX

GIFT City treasury centres: why Indian companies are moving treasury and borrowing there, and whether it's worth it.

GIFT City sat almost unused for four years. However, in the last eight months seven companies have taken a treasury licence, with seventeen more queuing.

So what changed?

This episode asks why two-thirds of India's external commercial borrowing suddenly routes through GIFT City, when a year ago it was a third. Why borrowing there is cheaper — and whether that's really about tax. Whether your balances can earn there, and whether you can simply move money in from a Mumbai account. And if you're weighing up your own treasury centre, why India rather than Singapore or Amsterdam.

Personal views. Not investment advice.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

GIFT City opened for corporate treasury centres in 2021. Then, for nearly four years, almost nothing happened.

Then seven companies took a licence in the last eight months. There are seventeen more in the queue behind them. And JPMorgan said in July that it's in conversations with over a hundred multinationals about doing the same thing.

So something changed. And I've spent the last few days working out what, because the reason turns out to be quite specific.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes a day for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

Now, licences are one signal, and honestly it's the smaller one. Here's the bigger one.

Last financial year, about a third of India's external commercial borrowing was routed through GIFT City. In the first nine months of this year, it was more than two-thirds.

So in the space of twelve months, GIFT City went from being the route that some companies took, to being the route that most companies take. That's eighteen billion dollars of borrowing this year alone, and around fifty-five billion cumulatively. There are thirty-five banking units operating there now, holding something like a hundred billion dollars between them.

And I'll be honest with you, I've been sceptical about GIFT City for years, and I suspect a lot of you have been too. It has been about to take off for most of a decade. But a market share that moves from a third to two-thirds in a single year isn't just a press release. That's a real shift in where companies are choosing to borrow.

So the obvious question is why. Why is it cheaper to borrow there.

And the answer isn't tax, which is what most people assume. It's about how banks are regulated.

A banking unit inside GIFT City doesn't carry India's reserve requirements, and it doesn't carry the priority sector lending obligations either. A domestic bank does, and that cost sits behind every loan it writes. The unit in GIFT City simply doesn't have it, and the difference comes through in the rate you're quoted.

There's also no cap on what those units can charge, so they price using the same global benchmarks your offshore lenders use. There's no stamp duty on transactions, and no tax on financial services either.

Now, I think the important thing about all of that is what it isn't. It isn't an incentive. It's not a subsidy that a finance minister can withdraw in next year's budget. The advantage comes from the way these units are structured, and that makes it a good deal more durable than a tax holiday.

Now, there's one thing I want to be really clear about, because I see it confused constantly. And I'll admit, I had it wrong myself until fairly recently.

The question people usually ask is whether their money can actually earn something there. And the answer is yes, it can. Banking units in GIFT City offer foreign currency accounts — savings accounts, current accounts and fixed deposits. Bank of Baroda has been running one since 2017, and the minimum deposit is somewhere around ten thousand dollars.

But here's where the confusion comes in. That is not the same thing as sweeping money out of your Mumbai account.

Under FEMA, GIFT City is treated as foreign territory. Everything that happens inside it is a non-resident transaction. The business is conducted in foreign currency, and rupees are only permitted for running costs — things like salaries and statutory fees. And that's deliberate. It's precisely what keeps GIFT City an offshore centre rather than a domestic tax shelter.

So when you move money there, you're not transferring between two of your own accounts. You're doing a cross-border transaction, and it needs a proper route. An Indian parent can fund a treasury unit through the overseas investment route. A subsidiary can sweep into one, provided it meets the FEMA conditions. Both of those work, and companies do use them. But they're structuring decisions that need advice. They're not something you configure in your cash management portal.

One thing did open up recently, though, and I think it's worth knowing about. That changed in October. Exporters can now hold foreign currency accounts in the IFSC for realising their export proceeds. So if you're sitting on dollar receivables, that's about as direct as it gets. You can park them there and earn on them, rather than converting into a rupee current account that pays you nothing at all.

So let me finish with what I think the practical takeaway is. Because there are actually two separate decisions here, and people tend to run them together.

The first decision is whether to borrow through GIFT City. And that's the easy one. Two-thirds of the market has already made it. You don't need to set up an entity. You don't need people on the ground in Gandhinagar. You just need a lender that has a banking unit there, and a reason to prefer their pricing.

So if you're raising foreign currency debt this year, ask a GIFT City bank to quote for it. Put that quote next to the one from your offshore lender and compare them. If you haven't been doing that, there's a fair chance you've been paying more than you needed to.

The second decision is whether to set up your own treasury centre. And that one is genuinely harder. The regulator widened what these centres are allowed to do last April, so they can now hedge, issue guarantees, and operate as a proper in-house bank, rather than just pooling cash. And that's really what's created the queue.

But if you're thinking about your own centre, the comparison isn't GIFT City versus doing nothing. It's GIFT City versus Singapore, or Amsterdam, or Dubai. So let me spend a minute on that, because it's the question sitting underneath all of this.

The first reason is cost, and it's the biggest one by some distance.

Office space is the easy part. Space in GIFT City runs around sixty per cent cheaper than Singapore, and across the whole cost base you're looking at something like thirty to forty per cent below the established hubs.

But rent isn't really the story here. The story is payroll.

Now, I looked into how big these centres actually are, because I'd assumed hundreds of people. They're not. The regulatory minimum at GIFT City is five qualified professionals, and Singapore's equivalent is three. In practice most treasury centres run lean — a small core team, with the volume work pushed out to a shared service centre somewhere else.

But the payroll gap is still where the money is. A treasury manager in Singapore averages around a hundred thousand Singapore dollars a year. The same role in India averages about twenty-six lakh rupees. That's close to three times the cost for the same job, and the gap widens as you go more senior.

And here's what matters most. A lot of groups already have their shared service centre in India, with hundreds of people in it. Put the treasury centre there too, and the whole finance stack sits in one cost base, in one time zone, under one management line. That's a bigger argument than the rent.

The second reason is tax, and it's changed recently in a way I think gets missed.

There's now a global agreement that large multinationals should pay at least fifteen per cent tax somewhere. And if one country charges you less than that, another country can collect the difference. So parking profit in a zero-tax location doesn't really work any more. You don't get to keep it.

Which is why what India did in this year's budget is interesting. The tax holiday for GIFT City units was extended to twenty years. And when that holiday ends, income is taxed at fifteen per cent. Exactly fifteen. That's the global floor, and it has clearly been set there on purpose, so the benefit stays with you instead of being collected somewhere else.

The third reason is smaller, and it's time zones. The Indian working day overlaps properly with Asia in the morning and Europe in the afternoon, which Singapore does less well. If you're running one hub for both regions, that's worth something. For the Americas it doesn't really help either way, so I wouldn't build a case on it.

And I should give you the other side, because there is one. Singapore has the deeper treaty network, a far longer regulatory track record, and a credibility in arbitration that GIFT City simply hasn't earned yet. If predictability matters more to you than cost, that's a real argument. And it's why plenty of groups will stay exactly where they are.

So the test, in the end, isn't the tax rate. The test is whether you actually have multi-currency activity that's worth relocating. And that means three things. First, real foreign currency balances. Second, lending between group companies across borders. And third, hedging that's being executed somewhere else today. If that's what your treasury looks like, then this became a lot more attractive last year.

And if it isn't — if your treasury is essentially rupees, and essentially domestic — then a team of five people in Gandhinagar is an expensive answer to a question you don't have.

So who's actually gone?

ArcelorMittal has licences for two centres, and has said it'll run India cash pooling the way it runs treasury out of Paris. Indian Oil has an entity there. So does ONGC Videsh. Adani is setting one up, and so is Bharti Airtel. Genpact is preparing, and ZF Friedrichshafen is looking at it without having applied yet.

Now look at that list again. Indian state-owned oil. Indian private conglomerates. A European steelmaker. A German industrial company. An American-listed services business.

There's no single profile there. Not one sector, not one size bracket, not one tax position explains it. And I think that's the most telling thing about all of this. When a trend is driven by one industry, it's a fashion. When it turns up across five completely different kinds of company at the same time, it's usually because something structural has shifted underneath them.

That's it for today. Sources are all in the show notes.

I'll see you tomorrow.

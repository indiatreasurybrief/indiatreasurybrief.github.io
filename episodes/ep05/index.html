Rupee outlook: three regimes for the rupee, and we're in the third

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Rupee & FX

Rupee outlook: the three regimes the rupee has moved through, why we're in the third, and what it means for companies with dollar exposure.

The rupee fell 2.9% in 2024. It fell 4.9% in 2025 — the worst performance of any currency in Asia. In the first five months of this year, it fell 7%.

Nothing about India got three times more fragile in eighteen months. What changed is that somebody stopped holding the currency up, and had told us in advance this was coming.

This episode takes the rupee in three parts, each a different regime. How it was held for most of the last decade, at a cost of hundreds of billions of dollars in reserves. How that changed with a new central bank governor in December 2024, and what the currency then did — a record low in February 2025, a new all-time low in May 2026. And what's holding it up now, which is different again: not spot dollar sales, but borrowed ones, through swap windows with published end dates.

It ends on what's actually ours to check — not where the currency goes next, but whether our hedge coverage, the instrument behind it, and the forecast it's built on were ever updated for the regime we're actually in.

Including the honest caveat: letting a currency move freely is a defensible policy, not a failure, and two of the three forces pushing the rupee down aren't Indian policy at all.

Second, how it was let go. That covers 2025 and the first half of this year.

And third, what's holding it up now, which is different again, and really only dates from June.

Those are three separate regimes. And I think a lot of us are still running policies that were written for the first one.

So, how it was held.

For most of the last decade, the rupee barely moved. And I mean that quite precisely. Its volatility was among the lowest of any emerging market currency. If you ran a treasury in India, that was simply the weather. The rupee drifted down two or three per cent a year, and you could plan around it.

That wasn't the market being kind. The Reserve Bank built up more than seven hundred billion dollars of reserves, and it used them. Whenever the currency moved faster than the Bank wanted, it sold dollars and slowed it down.

So the stability that most of our policies assume was real. It was just being paid for, by somebody else, on our behalf.

Now the second part. How it was let go.

In December of 2024, the Reserve Bank got a new Governor. And within weeks, the reporting was that he was open to letting the currency move more freely than his predecessor had.

He's been consistent about it since. The way he puts it is that intervention is there to maintain orderliness and stability without compromising market efficiency. And that it aims to smooth excessive and disruptive volatility, rather than target any specific exchange rate level or band.

Think about that carefully, because it's a real change. Smoothing volatility and defending a level are not the same policy. The first one slows a fall down. The second one stops it.

And the traders picked it up almost immediately. What they described was a tactical shift. The Bank still stepping in to prevent runaway moves, but allowing much wider ranges within the day.

Then look at what the currency actually did.

In January, the month after the December handover, the rupee fell three and a half per cent. In the eight months before it, from April to November, it had fallen one point three.

By February of 2025 it hit a record low of eighty-seven fifty-seven. Across 2025 as a whole it fell nearly five per cent, which made it the worst performer in Asia. Foreign investors pulled a record one point six six lakh crore rupees out of Indian equities that year, which is about nineteen billion dollars.

And then this year was faster again. Ninety-four seventy-one in March. Ninety-five thirty-three at the end of April, the day after Brent crude jumped seven per cent in a single session to a hundred and eleven dollars. And an all-time low of ninety-six eighty-two on the twentieth of May.

Which brings us to the third part. What's holding it up now.

And this is the part I find most interesting, because the tactics changed twice inside six months.

Through the first half of this year, the Bank was defending the rupee the expensive way. It was selling dollars it already owned.

You can see what that cost in the reserve numbers. In February, reserves were at a peak of seven hundred and twenty-eight billion dollars. By the first of May they were down to six hundred and ninety. That's forty billion dollars in about a month, and eleven billion of it inside a single week. Spot sales for the financial year came in at a record, somewhere around fifty-three billion dollars.

But you can't run that for long. Selling reserves is the most visible way there is to defend a currency, because everybody watching can count what's left. And it wasn't working. By the twentieth of May the rupee had hit its all-time low anyway.

So in June, the Bank changed the tool.

Instead of selling dollars it owned, it started borrowing them. It opened a window for foreign currency deposits from non-residents — money from Indians living abroad — and let the banks swap those dollars with the Reserve Bank at a concessional rate. A parallel window did the same thing for overseas corporate borrowing.

And it moved quickly. By July, the Reserve Bank's forward book had jumped thirty-three billion dollars in one month, to a record of a hundred and thirty-six billion. That's the other side of those swaps. The dollars come in, and the promise to give them back goes on the book.

By the middle of August, fifty-two billion dollars had arrived through the deposit window alone. And on the twenty-first of August, reserves hit a lifetime high. Seven hundred and twenty-nine billion dollars, which is higher than the February peak.

Now, here's the part I'd hold on to. The Bank did not buy a single dollar in the market to get there. It borrowed them, with an agreement to hand them back on a date in the future.

Then at the end of August it closed that window early, because it had run hotter than expected. Fresh deposits stopped getting the concessional rate from the first of September. The corporate borrowing window is still open, and it runs until the thirty-first of December.

So where does all that leave the currency? The rupee bottomed at ninety-six eighty-two in May. It's around ninety-five now. And on Wednesday it went back through that level as crude climbed again, with the Bank selling dollars into it.

I'd be careful about saying the windows caused the recovery. Those two things happened over the same months, and that's a sequence rather than a proof. But the order of events is worth knowing.

So what does any of this actually mean for us?

I think it comes down to one thing, and it isn't a view on the currency.

The first thing I'd check, especially if we're an importer, is how much of what we actually owe in dollars is covered right now, and whether that's still enough. A fifty per cent hedge ratio meant something different when the rupee moved two or three per cent a year than it does in a currency that's moved seven per cent in five months. The size of what's left uncovered is simply bigger now, even if the percentage on the page hasn't changed.

It's also worth checking whether the instrument still fits, not just the amount. A forward locks in a rate. It protects us if the rupee falls further, but we give up the benefit if it doesn't. An option costs more upfront, but gives that same protection while still letting us benefit if the rupee holds or recovers. That trade-off is worth more than it used to be, the more a currency can move.

The second thing is more basic than any hedge ratio. How confident are we in what we're actually going to receive and pay, in dollars, over the next few months? Most hedging only works as well as the forecast behind it.

When the rupee moved two or three per cent a year, a forecast that was a little off on timing or amount didn't matter much. In a currency that's done seven per cent in five months, a payment landing a month late, or turning out bigger or smaller than planned, is no longer a rounding error. It's exactly the kind of gap that decides whether a hedge actually protects us, or just looks like it does on paper.

And the third thing I'd note is the calendar. The deposit window closed early, at the end of August. The corporate borrowing window runs to the thirty-first of December. Whatever those are doing for the rupee, they aren't permanent, and both of them have published end dates. That's not a forecast, it's a diary entry.

Now, before I close, the honest caveat. And on this one it's substantial.

Letting a currency find its own level is a perfectly respectable policy. It isn't a failure. The standard argument against holding a currency is that you store the pressure up and release it all at once, which is worse for everybody. A lot of serious people think what India has done here is right.

And two of the three things pushing the rupee down aren't Indian policy at all. Crude above a hundred dollars is not something the Reserve Bank controls. Nor is a US Federal Reserve keeping rates higher for longer and holding the dollar up with it.

It may also be working. The rupee is stronger than it was in May. Reserves are at a record. Volatility is being smoothed rather than suppressed, which is exactly what was promised.

And borrowed reserves are still reserves. They're deployable, the obligations mostly sit beyond a year, and a central bank can roll a position in a way that no company could.

So I'm not telling you this is a problem. Whether it's better policy is above my pay grade, and I'd distrust anybody on a podcast who claimed otherwise.

But none of that changes the part that's ours. Whether the new regime is better isn't our question. The question is whether our own numbers still reflect it.

And who is this not for? If we've got no imports, no exports, no dollar debt and no overseas subsidiary, then this is background and nothing more.

So, to close.

The rupee was never a stable currency. It was a held one. And the holding was a choice, made by an institution that told us in advance it planned to stop.

What's holding it up today is different again. Borrowed dollars, on windows with dates on them.

None of that tells us where the currency goes next. But it does tell us that the cover we're carrying, and the forecast behind it, were both built for a world that ended in December of 2024.

I'd go and check both.

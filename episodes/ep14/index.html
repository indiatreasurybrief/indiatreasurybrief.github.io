RBI rules on AI and model risk: how treasury can borrow them

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Tech & operations · Regulation

RBI rules on AI and model risk: what the draft guidance asks of banks, and how treasury can borrow it for its own models.

The RBI's draft rules say a spreadsheet becomes a model the moment its answer sets a price or drives a decision. By that test, most corporate treasury desks in India run models too.

The Reserve Bank of India's draft guidance on model risk management would require banks and other lenders to list every model they use, have each one checked independently, plan fallbacks, and build kill switches for AI. This episode looks at what that changes in how banks deal with corporate treasury, and how treasury can borrow a lighter version for its own spreadsheets and AI tools.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

In August, the Governor of the Reserve Bank stood in front of India's bankers and told them that "the model decided" is never an acceptable answer, whether it's given to a customer, to an auditor, or to the Reserve Bank itself. His own regulator is now writing that into rules for banks, and the draft has a definition in it that I think every treasurer should hear. A spreadsheet that works out a loan price, it says, is only a basic calculator, right up until somebody uses it to actually set the price. From that moment, the Reserve Bank says, it counts as a model, and it needs the same checks as any other model, whether or not the bank calls it one. Now, on a treasury desk, the cash forecast, the hedging sheet and the bank limits all drive decisions about real money. So by the Reserve Bank's own test, each of them is a model too. Nobody's going to make us check them the way banks have to check theirs, and that's exactly why I'd borrow the banks' rules and use them on ourselves.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

In the last episode, I said the next one would be about export receivables. That one's still coming, now with the Reserve Bank's new answers on the reserve built in. Today's is about AI, and about the rulebook the banks are getting for it.

So let's start with what the banks are being told.

In June, the Reserve Bank put out a draft on what it calls model risk. The consultation closed in July, and the final version isn't out yet, so everything I'm about to describe could still change. But the shape of it is clear. It covers banks, finance companies and most of the other lenders the Reserve Bank supervises, and it applies to every model they use, whether they built it, bought it, or never thought of it as a model at all.

The rules themselves are fairly simple to say. The board has to approve a framework. The bank has to keep a list of every model it uses, and it has to keep a model on that list for ten years after it's retired. Every model has to be tested by someone other than the person who built it. That's true even when the model comes from an outside supplier, because the bank is still responsible for it. There has to be a fallback, which the draft describes as manual intervention or a backup arrangement, for the day a model fails. And for AI models in particular, there has to be a way to override them, suspend them, or switch them off. The draft actually uses the words kill switch.

It also replaces the Reserve Bank's guidance on credit risk models, which is more than twenty years old. So this isn't a side note about chatbots. It reaches the models that decide who gets credit, and on what terms.

Why is the Reserve Bank doing this now? Part of the answer is in a survey it ran last year. It asked six hundred and twelve of the firms it supervises how they were using AI. About one in five said they were using it or building it. That's lower than the conference talk would suggest. But here's the part that caught my eye. Of the firms already using AI, fewer than one in five kept audit logs of what it did, and only about one in seven ran regular audits. So even inside regulated banks, the use came first, and the checking hasn't caught up.

And on Thursday, the Financial Stability Board, which brings together central banks and regulators from the biggest economies, published its final version of twelve practices for banks and other financial firms using AI. They're not binding. But they cover the same ground: oversight from the board, real human supervision, being able to explain what a model does, and managing the risk of relying on outside AI providers.

So when you put all of that together, the Reserve Bank isn't worried that banks use models. It's worried that nobody can say which models they use, who checks them, or how to switch one off. And honestly, most treasuries couldn't answer those three questions either. Nobody's going to ask us. That's exactly why I'd ask them ourselves.

Before we get to our own models, what does all of this change in how the banks deal with us?

Let me be honest about this part, because it's smaller than you might think. The draft doesn't mention corporate customers at all. And for a large company, a credit decision already comes from a rating model and a credit committee, with people in the room. That isn't about to change.

But I'd expect three things. The first is about explanations. If a bank has to be able to explain a model's decision to its auditor and to the Reserve Bank, then it should be able to explain it to us. So when a limit gets cut or a price moves, I think it's perfectly fair to ask what drove it, and who looked at it. The Governor's line cuts both ways.

The second is that banks will probably ask us for more data. In that same speech, the Governor's examples of what AI can do in lending included reading cash flows and tax filings to judge smaller borrowers. A model that runs on data needs data, and the requests tend to arrive as paperwork for the customer. I'd expect that to land hardest on mid sized companies.

And the third is that when a bank's portal or a chat service uses AI to deal with customers, the draft says it has to tell us that, and it has to let us reach a human when we ask. That's a small thing, but it's worth knowing it's there.

So, now for the part that I think matters most for us and our treasury desks, which is our own models.

Let's go back to that definition. If a spreadsheet only does sums, it's a calculator. If we use its answer to make a decision, it's a model. Now let's think about what runs a treasury desk. There's the cash forecast that decides how much we borrow and how much we invest. There's the sheet that tells us what share of next quarter's imports to hedge. There's the exposure calculator, and the one that tracks how much we've got with each bank. By the Reserve Bank's own test, every one of those is a model.

And that's most of us. A survey of Indian treasury leaders last year found that more than seven in ten still depend on spreadsheets. About a quarter had started AI pilots, and more than eight in ten expected AI to be critical within a year or two, with cash forecasting as the first place they'd use it.

Now, I should be careful here. It isn't true that nobody looks at our spreadsheets. If we're a listed company, the auditors test the controls behind our financial reporting, so the sheet that values our hedges for the accounts does get looked at. But no regulator asks us to govern a cash forecast, or the sheet that decides how much we hedge, and those are the ones that move money.

Last week showed why that matters. On the tenth of October, the hedging rules changed. Any sheet that works out what import cover costs, if it was built before that date, leaves out the new reserve. Bankers have put that cost somewhere between eighty and a hundred and fifty basis points, and the Reserve Bank's answers this week confirmed the banks earn no interest on the reserve, so the cost is real. And any rule of thumb that says we can cancel a hedge and book it again later is simply wrong now. Nothing was wrong with the maths in those sheets. The world changed underneath them. A model that somebody owns gets updated that week. A model that nobody owns keeps giving answers, and we keep trusting them.

AI raises the stakes, for two reasons. It's harder to explain why an AI forecast says what it says. And the claims around it are very confident, with some of the material aimed at treasurers promising forecast accuracy that I'd want to see tested on our own numbers first. The newer tools don't just advise either. Some of them are built to act, and a tool that can act is exactly the kind the Reserve Bank wants banks to be able to switch off.

So before an AI tool's output goes into a real decision, or into a board pack, I'd want four answers in writing. Can we explain what it's telling us? What do we do by hand if it fails? Who's allowed to switch it off? And what does the contract say about our right to check it, and about where our data goes? That last one matters more from May twenty twenty seven, when most of the new data protection rules take full effect, so I'd want to know whether anything we feed into a tool includes personal data, like employee or supplier bank details.

Now, there's a fair argument that this is overkill. The draft itself calls a spreadsheet calculator a basic tool. Banks need all of this because they're holding depositors' money, and a mistake in one big bank's model can spread across the system. A corporate treasury isn't that. And heavy paperwork is a very good way to kill the small pilots that might actually make forecasting better.

I agree with most of that, which is why I'd borrow a much lighter version, not the whole rulebook.

And the good news is that the Reserve Bank has already done the hard thinking for us. The draft is public, and it's written as a set of principles, so we can read it as a template. I'd take its main headings, which are a list of models, an owner for each one, a check by someone who didn't build it, a fallback for the day it fails, and a way to switch off anything that uses AI. Then I'd write one page under each, sized for a treasury rather than a bank. Where the draft asks a bank's board to approve the framework, our version could be signed off by the finance director or the audit committee. And because it borrows the regulator's own language, it's much easier to explain to our auditors, and to our banks, why we've done it this way.

So here's what I'd do. First, I'd write a one page list of every tool whose output moves money or a hedge. That's the cash forecast, the hedging and exposure sheets, the bank limits, and any AI feature inside our treasury system or a bank's portal. Each one gets a named owner, and the date it was last checked. And I'd start with anything that still assumes we can cancel and rebook, or that leaves out the reserve.

Second, for the handful of tools that actually drive decisions, and any AI pilot, I'd get those four answers before their output goes anywhere that matters.

And third, I'd ask each of our banks one question. When your model changes our limit or our price, who explains it to us?

If that list turns out to be short, and nothing on it really drives a decision, then that's the job done, and I'd stop there. But I'd be surprised. Most of us will find a few sheets that decide real money, with nobody quite sure who last checked them.

Because the Governor's line works for us as well as for the banks. "The model decided" isn't an answer we'd accept from a bank, and it isn't one our board should have to accept from us. A model we can't explain, or switch off, is a model we don't really control.

Will RBI hike rates in December? The case for and against

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Rates

Will the RBI hike rates again in December? Retail inflation crossed the RBI's 6% upper limit in October, and the RBI's monetary policy committee, which raised the repo rate in October for the first time in nearly four years, decides on 4 December.

We cover what economists and the bond market expect for the December meeting; the case for a second hike (the real rate, households' inflation expectations, wholesale prices) and the case against (oil, a steadier rupee, core inflation close to target); what could still move the decision; how fast a hike reaches bank loans linked to the repo rate and to MCLR; and what I'd do before the decision, including for SMEs.

In October, inflation crossed the RBI's upper limit, and the RBI says its next move can only be a hike or a pause. So will it raise rates again on the fourth of December? Most economists think so, but falling oil and a steadier rupee make the case for a pause. If it does, treasuries will feel it quickly, because about two thirds of floating rate bank loans reset within three months of a hike.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for India's treasury and finance teams, from big corporates to startups.

I'll cover three things. First, what the market expects on the fourth of December. Second, the case for a hike, and the case against. And third, what it means for our loans.

So, first, what the market expects.

October's retail inflation came in at six point one per cent, above the RBI's upper limit of six and a little higher than what economists had forecast. Food did most of the damage, with food inflation at nearly nine per cent and vegetables leading it.

A quick word on how that number is built. The RBI targets four per cent and can live with anything between two and six. In the new index that started this January, food is a little over a third of the basket, so one bad month for vegetables can push the whole number past the band. That's why the RBI also watches core inflation, which leaves out food and fuel, and I'll come back to it.

A December hike would be the second in a row. In October, the RBI's monetary policy committee raised the repo rate by twenty five basis points, the first rise in nearly four years, and the repo rate now stands at five point five per cent. The vote on the rate was unanimous. The vote on the stance was closer: four members moved it from neutral to what they called "calibrated tightening", and two wanted to stay neutral. And the minutes were clear that cuts are off the table.

Most economists now expect another twenty five basis points on the fourth of December. ICRA's chief economist, Aditi Nayar, called it "almost certain", and economists at DBS and IDFC First Bank expect a hike too. A few expect more, and Bloomberg Economics sees another hike in February.

Bond yields show how far the market has moved this year. The ten year government bond yield started the year below six point seven per cent and climbed to about seven point five per cent in October, its highest in four years, as oil and inflation rose. It eased to about seven point two per cent in November as oil fell, but it's still well above where it began.

For the year ahead, the market is pricing in more than most economists expect. In Reuters' September poll, economists' views on the whole cycle ranged from twenty five or fifty basis points of hikes in total to as much as a hundred. The swap market sits at the top of that range. In the middle of October, the one year swap rate was about six point five five per cent, which dealers read as roughly a hundred basis points of hikes over the next year. After the October inflation figure came out, traders were still betting on about that much. That gap matters when you decide what to fix, because a fixed rate is priced off the market's view, not the economists'.

So, second, the case for a hike, and the case against. I see three arguments on each side.

The first argument for a hike is the real rate. With the repo rate at five point five per cent and inflation just over six, the real policy rate is below zero. Even against the RBI's own forecast of about five point six per cent for April to June next year, it's roughly nil. With inflation above target, a negative real rate means policy is still loose, and that's the simplest case for going again.

The second argument is expectations. The RBI's own survey found that households expect inflation of almost ten per cent three months from now, and the committee cited it in its October minutes. What households expect tends to feed into wages and prices, and one member, Poonam Gupta, wrote that a further increase "might be needed" to keep those expectations anchored.

The third argument is costs in the pipeline. In the same minutes, another member, Saugata Bhattacharya, pointed out that wholesale inflation was running at nearly nine per cent. Input costs like that tend to reach retail prices with a lag, which argues for acting before they show up.

Now the three arguments against, or at least for a smaller move.

The first argument against is oil. Brent has fallen by about a fifth since its peak in late October, on hopes that the Iran war is ending. On the tenth of November, it briefly dipped below ninety dollars a barrel for the first time since August. It has bounced back a little since, after mines hit tankers near the Strait of Hormuz, but cheaper oil still feeds into fuel and freight costs, and it eases the import bill.

The second argument against is the rupee. In October, the committee listed the rupee's fall as a risk to inflation. Since then, the rupee has recovered from its record low near ninety seven to under ninety four per dollar. Because the rupee has steadied, IDFC First Bank's economist, Gaura Sengupta, sees no need for more than twenty five basis points.

The third argument against is core inflation. By the estimates of IDFC First Bank and Bloomberg Economics, core inflation was about four point five per cent in October, close to the RBI's four per cent target. The headline number also came in roughly where the RBI expected, since its own forecast for October to December was six per cent. Rate hikes work on demand, and they don't bring down the price of vegetables.

Growth cuts both ways. The economy grew seven point eight per cent in April to June, which gives the RBI room to tighten. But the RBI's own forecasts have growth slowing over the rest of the year. Two of the six members wanted to stay neutral in October, and one of them, Ram Singh, argued for "mild" hikes, not rates kept high for long.

Three things could still move the decision. The first is growth, because the figures for July to September come out on the thirtieth of November, and the finance ministry's early estimate is a little over seven per cent. A strong number makes a hike easier, and a weak one strengthens the case for a pause. The second is oil, which is moving with every headline from the Iran talks. The third is the rupee. November's inflation number only comes after the meeting, so October's is the last one the committee will see.

So where does that leave us? Most economists expect twenty five basis points, a few expect more, and the case for a pause rests mostly on oil and the rupee. I won't guess which way the committee goes. But I'd say the bigger question for treasuries is where rates settle over the next year, and the market is already pricing in more than one hike.

So, third, what it means for our loans.

A rate hike isn't news about the Reserve Bank. It's a line in our own budget, and for most loans it arrives within three months.

That's because of how bank loans are priced now. Since October twenty nineteen, new floating rate loans to micro and small firms have had to be linked to an external benchmark, usually the repo rate, and reset at least every three months. Medium sized firms followed in April twenty twenty. By the RBI's count, about two thirds of all floating rate bank loans are now on an external benchmark, and at private banks it's over ninety per cent. So when the repo rate moves, these loans move within a quarter. After the October hike, SBI raised its repo linked lending rate by the full twenty five basis points within about a week.

Most of the rest are on MCLR, and an MCLR loan reprices only on its reset date, often once a year, with banks tending to pass on less. After October, SBI's one year MCLR rose by fifteen basis points, against the full twenty five on its repo linked rate.

Market borrowing works differently again. Commercial paper and bonds are fixed until they mature, so a hike only reaches you when you roll over or issue again. But the bond market has already moved ahead of the RBI, so that next issue is dearer.

So here's what I'd do.

First, before the fourth of December, I'd map every loan by its benchmark, whether that's the repo rate, a treasury bill rate or MCLR, along with its spread and its next reset date. That tells you how much of the interest bill moves within three months, and how much waits for a reset.

Second, I'd budget for a range rather than a single number, and show the board next year's interest cost under a pause, one more hike, or two more. As a rough guide, every twenty five basis points on a hundred crore rupees of floating debt costs about twenty five lakh rupees per year.

Third, I'd decide what to fix. For debt you'll carry for years, a fixed rate loan, a bond, or a swap where you pay fixed can lock in the cost, but swap rates already include the hikes the market expects. So fixing now means paying for those hikes whether they come or not, and if the RBI stops after one more, the floating borrower comes out ahead. That's a call each treasury has to make for itself, and I'd keep working capital floating either way.

And fourth, on the other side of the balance sheet, rising rates help, because short deposits, treasury bills and liquid funds pick up the higher rates as they roll over.

For a smaller company or a startup, the hike is more direct. If you're an SME with a floating bank loan, it's almost certainly linked to the repo rate by rule. So a December hike would reach you within about three months, and your cash credit and overdraft limits would move with it. I'd find the reset date and the spread in your sanction letter. And if you're a micro or small business, the rules are on your side. For floating rate loans taken or renewed since January this year, most banks can no longer charge a penalty for paying off early. So if another bank offers a tighter spread, moving is a real option.

Whatever the committee decides on the fourth of December, the loans that move are already written into your agreements. Knowing which ones, before the decision, is the part we control.

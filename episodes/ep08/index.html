Fed rate hike and the rupee forward premium: what changed

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Rupee & FX · Rates

Fed rate hike and the rupee: what the Fed's first rise since 2023 did to the rupee forward premium and the cost of hedging.

The US Federal Reserve raised interest rates on 16 September, its first increase since 2023, and its own projections no longer show cuts next year.

This episode looks at what that means for corporate treasury in India: the Federal Reserve's own votes and projections, oil prices after the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, inflation, the Indian rupee and its forward premium, the Reserve Bank of India's currency swaps and forward book, Indian interest rates before the October monetary policy decision, and hedging decisions on imports, exports and dollar loans.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

At the start of this year, the debate inside the Fed was about how many more times to cut rates. On the sixteenth of September, the twelve people who set US interest rates voted to raise them instead. Normally, when American interest rates go up, hedging dollar payments should get cheaper. This time the cost has gone up, and by quite a lot. And I think the reason tells us more about the rupee than it does about the Fed.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

In this episode, I want to walk through how the Fed changed its mind so quickly, why our hedging cost didn't follow it, and what I'd do about it on the desk.

Start with the vote itself, because it tells the story better than any forecast. Twelve people vote on US interest rates: the Fed's governors in Washington, plus regional presidents who take turns.

Last December, the Fed cut rates, and even then the committee was split. Three members voted against the cut. One wanted a bigger cut, and the others didn't want a cut at all. That's roughly where things stood as this year began. The argument was about the pace of cuts, and not the direction.

Then the war with Iran started at the end of February. Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz all but stopped. Brent was around seventy dollars before the war. By the end of March it was a hundred and eighteen. For an economy where inflation had already been above target for five years, that's about the worst kind of shock to get.

You can see the committee move after that. In April, four of the twelve voted against the decision, the most divided the Fed had been in over thirty years. One of them still wanted a cut. The other three objected to the Fed even hinting that more cuts were coming. In July, those same three went further, and voted to raise rates, while the other nine said wait. And in September, all twelve voted to raise. It was the first increase in more than three years.

So in about nine months, the committee went from arguing over how fast to cut, to agreeing that it had to hike. And they don't think they're finished.

Most of them expect at least one more increase before the end of this year, and most don't see any cuts next year either.

There's also a new chair. Kevin Warsh took over in May, and he's said plainly that he isn't in the business of telling markets in advance what the Fed will do. For years, we've been used to the Fed signalling its next move well ahead of time. I'd expect a lot less of that now, so turns like this one could come with less warning.

Now let's bring this back to the rupee.

Most of us carry a simple rule of thumb about the forward premium. It's roughly the difference between Indian and US interest rates. So when the Fed raises rates, that difference narrows, and hedging a dollar payable should get a little cheaper.

This year, it hasn't worked that way. I went back to the official benchmark numbers for the forward premium, starting from last October, and compared them with interest rates in both countries.

The first surprise is that the difference in rates barely moved. US rates went up, but so did ours, because our own market started expecting the Reserve Bank to raise rates as well.

And yet the cost of hedging went up anyway. Last October, locking in dollars a year ahead cost around two and a third per cent. Just before the Fed's decision, it was about three and a third. That's a full percentage point more, at a time when the rule of thumb said it should be getting cheaper.

So where is the extra coming from? I think it's three things, and none of them has much to do with the Fed.

The first is simply who's buying protection. As the rupee fell this year, importers rushed to hedge, and exporters held back, hoping to sell their dollars at a better rate later. One analysis of clearing data found that in the first four months of the year, importers booked roughly twice as much forward cover as exporters did. When far more people want to buy dollars forward than sell them, that protection gets more expensive. You could see it work the other way in June, when the rupee recovered, exporters came back in record numbers, and the premium eased.

The second is the Reserve Bank itself. In June, it opened a special window for banks to raise dollar deposits from non resident Indians, and it picked up the hedging cost itself. More than a hundred and twenty billion dollars came in, and the premium dropped quickly. But all those dollars also left the banking system with a very large surplus of rupees. So in September, the Reserve Bank started draining that surplus through currency swaps, and one side-effect was that the premium jumped again. The one month premium rose by more than a percentage point in a single day. I don't think the Reserve Bank minds that much. A higher premium makes it more expensive to bet against the rupee.

The third is the size of the Reserve Bank's own position. It has now committed to selling close to a hundred and forty billion dollars in the forward market, which is a record. That makes it the biggest single player in the market our hedges are priced from. So the biggest position in the market we price our hedges off isn't a bank. It's the Reserve Bank.

So I think the honest summary is this. The Fed changed its mind, but what we pay to protect ourselves against the rupee is being set much closer to home, by who's hedging, and by what the Reserve Bank needs the rupee to do. For years, watching the Fed gave us a decent read on our hedging cost. This year, that shortcut stopped working.

So what is the premium telling us?

On Indian rates, it's telling us the market has already made up its mind. The one year swap rate is now above six per cent, against a repo rate of five and a quarter. Reuters reads that as at least three rate increases from the Reserve Bank priced in over the next year. The next decision is on the seventh of October, and I'm not going to guess what it'll be. But if the Reserve Bank does raise rates the way the market expects, the gap with US rates widens again, and that pushes the cost of hedging up, not down. So I wouldn't budget for hedging costs falling back to last year's levels.

On the rupee, one thing is worth being clear about. A forward rate isn't a forecast of where the rupee is going. Most of it is just the interest rate difference. But the extra we're paying on top of that is worth watching, because it has moved almost in step with the rupee all year.

Three moves this year. It rose as the rupee fell to its record low in May. It eased when the rupee steadied over the summer. And it's been climbing again this month. So I'd read it as a measure of how nervous the market is, and not as a prediction.

So what would I actually do? There are four things.

First, imports. I'd be deliberate about how far out we hedge. A lot of that nervousness is already in today's price, and the cost isn't even across the curve. Just before the Fed's decision, hedging three to ten months out cost more per year than hedging a full year. So it's worth choosing where on the curve we buy, and asking whether an option or a natural hedge would do the job better than simply hedging more.

Second, exports. I'd decide whether to hedge, rather than drift into not hedging. Selling dollars a year forward now earns about a point more than it did last October. Holding back is a bet that the rupee keeps falling, and it gives up that extra. That's fine if we've chosen it. It's expensive if it's just habit.

Third, our floating rate dollar loans. Most of those are priced off SOFR, the US benchmark rate. If our budget for this year was built in March or April, it assumed a Fed that was still cutting, so I'd rerun the interest cost on today's outlook. And I'd take the hedging question to the board as a policy decision: a swap, a cap, or staying floating. Since February, the rules on foreign currency borrowing don't require a hedge at all, so nobody else is making that call for us.

And fourth, I'd watch the Reserve Bank at least as closely as the Fed. Its swaps, its forward position and its October decision now move our hedging cost more directly than anything said in Washington.

It's also worth saying that all of this could reverse quickly. If the Strait reopens and oil falls, the Fed's thinking could turn again, and the premium has already shown it can drop fast. In April, it came down by more than half a point in a couple of days. So an importer who rushes to lock in a full year now, or an exporter who panics into selling forward, could regret it within weeks. I don't think the answer is to hedge more, or to hedge less. It's to recognise who's actually setting the price now, and to make the decision on that basis.

For years, we've watched the Fed to work out what our hedges would cost. The Fed has turned, and we'll feel that in our dollar loans. But what we pay to hedge the rupee is now being decided a lot closer to home.

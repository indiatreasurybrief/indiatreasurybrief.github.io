Mega IPOs and India's money market: why they didn't move it

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Cash & liquidity · Markets & economy

Mega IPOs and India's money market: why the big share sales didn't drain liquidity, and what actually moved short-term rates.

The National Stock Exchange's ₹22,562 crore share sale closed on 21 September, and Jio Platforms has filed for one larger still. Neither is doing much to India's money market.

This episode looks at what that means for corporate treasury in India: why application money is blocked rather than paid away, the cap on borrowing to bid, the Reserve Bank's standing deposit facility and its reverse repo auctions, the ₹1 lakh crore of open market bond sales running through September, what allotment day actually moves between banks, and how I'd time a commercial paper roll or a surplus placement around all of it.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

On the twenty first of September, bidding closed on the National Stock Exchange's own share sale, one of the biggest this country has ever seen, with about ninety thousand crore rupees of bids sitting in bank accounts. Back in twenty ten, a share sale on that scale could squeeze the whole banking system. This time, the overnight call rate finished the day a basis point under the repo rate. And I think the reason it didn't move tells us what's really setting the price of our money now.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

In this episode, I want to look at why a share sale this big used to be a money market event, why it isn't any more, and what I'd watch instead.

Start with the issues everyone's talking about. The National Stock Exchange is selling about twenty two and a half thousand crore rupees of shares. All of it is existing shareholders selling, so the exchange itself doesn't receive any of the money, and the shares list on the twenty fourth. Behind it is Jio Platforms, which filed its papers in June for an issue expected to be around thirty seven thousand crore. That would be the largest share sale India has ever had, although the timing still isn't settled.

Almost everything written about these issues has been about the stock market. Will fund managers sell other shares to make room? Will smaller companies get squeezed? Those are fair questions, but they aren't the ones a treasury desk asks. Ours is simpler. If we're placing surplus cash or rolling commercial paper over the next couple of weeks, does any of this change the rate?

I went in half expecting the answer to be yes, at least for a day or so, because that's what I remembered. And the memory isn't wrong. It's just from a different system.

In October twenty ten, Coal India raised about fifteen thousand crore, the biggest share sale the country had seen at the time, and it was heavily oversubscribed. Back then, applying for shares meant the money actually left your account, and it sat with the issue until allotment and refunds were done. One report at the time said the banking system ran short that week, mainly because of Coal India.

So the instinct that a big share sale drains the system is a real memory. But three things have changed since, and together they mean the same event barely registers now.

Now, the first change is how the money moves, and I think it's the one most people haven't noticed.

When you apply for shares today, your money isn't taken. It's blocked in your own bank account, where it stays as a deposit, and only the amount you're actually allotted gets debited. The rest is simply released. That's been compulsory for institutions and wealthy investors since twenty eleven, and for everyone since twenty sixteen. On allotment day, the money that does move goes from the applicants' banks to the sellers' banks. It changes which bank holds it, but it doesn't take it out. So the ninety thousand crore that looks like a drain was never really a drain. It was already sitting in the banks, and it stayed there.

The second change is borrowing. Before twenty twenty two, wealthy applicants could borrow from non bank lenders to bid, and by one estimate at the time they were putting up just one or two per cent of their own money and borrowing the rest. That's leverage of seventy or eighty times. And I think that's where the old pressure on short term rates really came from, because a blocked deposit is money that already sits inside a bank, while a loan of eighty times your stake is new money that a lender has to go and raise first. In April twenty twenty two, the Reserve Bank capped that lending at one crore per borrower, and it's still the rule. So that burst of borrowed money has largely gone.

And the third change is scale, which is where the real action is.

On the day bidding closed, banks put more than one and a half lakh crore rupees into the Reserve Bank's standing deposit facility. That's the floor. It pays a quarter of a point less than repo, and banks use it when they can't find anyone better to lend to. On top of that, they placed another seventy five thousand crore in a variable rate reverse repo auction. So on the day about ninety thousand crore of bids was sitting blocked, the banks handed the central bank roughly two and a half times that amount, because they had nothing better to do with it.

The price of money that day barely moved. The call rate finished at five point two four per cent, against a repo rate of five and a quarter, and tri party repo, which is where a lot of our surplus ends up through liquid funds, was lower again.

And the Reserve Bank isn't only mopping up. It's actively draining. This month it's offering a lakh crore of government bonds for sale in the open market. It held one of those sales on the seventeenth, the day bidding opened, and another on the twenty first, the day bidding closed.

So the share sale is the loudest thing on the screen, but the thing actually moving the water is the Reserve Bank, and it's doing it on the same days.

So is there anything left of the old effect? A little, and I want to be straight about it.

Allotment happens the day after bidding closes, and that's when money moves between banks. A smaller bank that loses deposits and doesn't get any back could be short for a day. You can even see a trace of that. On closing day, a little over four thousand crore was borrowed from the Reserve Bank at its penalty rate. That's small, and I wouldn't read much into it, but it's a reminder that the system can be flush overall and still have one bank short somewhere.

Jio, when it comes, will be bigger. But none of the three changes gets weaker with the size of the issue. The money still won't leave, the borrowing is still capped, and unless the surplus disappears, a bigger issue is still small next to what the Reserve Bank is moving.

That last condition is the one I'd actually watch. The surplus has come down a lot this month. Part of that is the Reserve Bank's own bond sales, and part is that mid September is when advance tax leaves the system. Even so, the surplus was still almost five lakh crore on the day bidding closed. But if the Reserve Bank keeps draining at this pace, short rates will firm, and I'd rather we saw that coming. It just won't be because of a share sale.

So what would I actually do? Three things.

First, when I'm timing a commercial paper roll or placing surplus over the next couple of weeks, I'd work off the Reserve Bank's calendar, and not the IPO calendar. Its next bond sale is due on the twenty eighth, and that's a more useful date to have in the diary than any listing.

Second, I'd read the Reserve Bank's daily money market release. It's published every working day, it's free, and it shows what the call rate did, how much went into the standing deposit facility, and how much was taken in the reverse repo auctions. How much banks are willing to leave at the floor is the best single reading I know of for how much slack is left. When that number starts to shrink, the slack is going, and that's when I'd expect short rates to move.

And third, I'd be sceptical of any story that says a share sale will tighten liquidity, unless it explains how the money actually leaves the banking system. Most of the time now, it doesn't.

It's worth saying how I could be wrong about this. Allotment day is the real test. If the call rate or tri party repo trades well above the repo rate in the days after allotment, then the old effect is still there, and I've got this wrong. So far, the numbers say otherwise.

For years, a big share sale was a money market event, and treasurers were right to plan around it. The rules changed, and the plumbing changed with them. What sets the price of our money now isn't the IPO calendar. It's the Reserve Bank.

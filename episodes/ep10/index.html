Tokenised corporate bonds in India: Demat 2.0 and the digital rupee

Apple PodcastsSpotifyYouTubeAny app

Topics: Tech & operations · Markets & economy

Tokenised corporate bonds in India: how Demat 2.0 and the wholesale digital rupee work, and whether a corporate treasury can take part.

The Securities and Exchange Board of India and the Reserve Bank of India have launched Demat 2.0, a pilot in which corporate bonds are issued as tokens and paid for in the wholesale digital rupee. REC, Larsen & Toubro and IIFL Finance issued the first bonds in September 2026.

This episode is about the digital rupee wallet at the centre of it: what the wallet is and how it works day to day, whether a corporate treasury can open one and invest, what changes for settlement and coupon payments, and how long the Reserve Bank's pilots have taken to move beyond the pilot stage.

The script this episode was narrated from. The narration is an AI voice.

On the ninth of September, Larsen and Toubro sold five hundred crore rupees of three year bonds, and just four investors bought the whole issue. L&T is one of the most familiar names in our bond market, and the offer document says companies like ours were allowed to apply. So I don't think the problem was demand. I think very few investors had set up what this bond needs, which is a new kind of wallet. It was one of India's first tokenised corporate bonds, and for a treasurer, the question isn't how the technology works. It's whether we'll be ready to buy the next one.

This is the India Treasury Brief — ten minutes, twice a week, for treasurers on what India's corporates are doing with their money.

In this episode, I want to look at what these bonds are, the wallet at the centre of them, whether a corporate treasury can get one, and how long this might take.

Start with what happened. In the first week of September, three companies sold bonds under a new pilot run by the Securities and Exchange Board of India, SEBI, together with the Reserve Bank. R.E.C., the state owned lender to the power sector, went first on the seventh, with five hundred crore. L&T followed on the ninth with another five hundred, and the non bank lender IIFL Finance raised a smaller twenty five crore the same day. There was real demand, too. R.E.C. drew bids of nearly eight hundred crore for its five hundred. The next day, SEBI and the Reserve Bank launched the pilot publicly at the Global Fintech Fest in Mumbai. They've called it Demat two-point-oh.

So what's actually different? Less than the name suggests, and I think that's the point.

First, the new pieces, because they're the whole story. The first is the ledger. A ledger is just a record of who owns what. Today the depositories keep that record in their own databases. In this pilot, it sits on a private, shared ledger that the depositories run, built on the technology most people know as blockchain, and the bond itself is written onto it as a token. It's not crypto, though. The bond is still an ordinary bond, and the ledger is run only by the depositories and the exchanges. The second piece is the money. The payment moves in the wholesale digital rupee, which is the version the Reserve Bank built for institutions, not the one people have on their phones. It's still a rupee, and it's still central bank money. It just moves as a digital token instead of through the usual bank payment rails. And to hold it, you need a wallet with your bank. I'll come back to that wallet, because it's where this gets interesting for us.

The bidding happens on the same electronic platform the exchanges already run for bond issues, on the same timelines. What's new is that the bond and the money are linked, so either both move, or neither does. The market calls that atomic settlement.

In a normal bond issue, investors pay in a day or so after bidding, and the bond shows up in their demat account after that. Somebody is always waiting on somebody. If we've ever paid for a bond and waited for it to arrive, we've carried that gap ourselves. Most days it doesn't matter at all. It matters on the one day the other side can't deliver. With the R.E.C. bond, the bids, the allotment, the payment and the listing all happened on the same day.

The same wiring changes what happens after we've bought. Today, when a coupon falls due, the issuer's registrar pulls a list of holders as of the record date, the payments go out through the banks, and somebody on our side checks what arrived against what should have. That's where things can slip. Here, the bond's terms are written into the token, the ledger itself is the list of holders, and the coupon goes straight to the wallet on the due date. The offer document for the L&T bond puts it simply. No action is required from the holder for redemption. For a treasury running a book of bonds, that's one less thing to chase on every coupon date.

Now, the wallet, because I think it's the real change here, and it's the part most of the coverage has skipped.

There are actually separate wallets, one for the bond and one for the money. The head of NSDL, one of the depositories, described them as a securities wallet and a currency wallet. The securities wallet holds the bond. It sits with the depository as an extension of the demat account we already have, so there's no new demat account and no fresh KYC, and the depository looks after the cryptographic keys, so nobody on our side has to. The currency wallet holds the digital rupee, and it's opened with our own bank. That's the one that matters.

Here's how it works. The money starts in our ordinary current account. When we need to pay, it moves into the wallet as digital rupees, settles against the bond, and anything left over goes back to the current account at the end of the day. The Reserve Bank's own figures show it. On the last day of March, the wholesale digital rupee outstanding was zero, because everything had swept back overnight. So the wallet isn't somewhere cash sits. It's more like a door that opens for the day.

And that door has to be open before the issue opens. The R.E.C. offer document says applicants must register on Demat two-point-oh, and enter their wallet details, before the issue opening date. So the wallet decides whether we can bid at all.

And I think there's a bigger point here. The same digital rupee is what the Reserve Bank is using for its other experiments. It's already running a pilot in tokenised certificates of deposit, and its annual report talks about widening these pilots to business uses and bringing in more participants. So a wallet opened for bonds could turn out to be the key to a lot more than bonds.

So can a corporate treasury actually get in? I went back to the offer documents to check. SEBI is clear that a tokenised bond is the same bond, so if the ordinary version is a permissible investment for us, so is this one. And the offer document for the L&T bond lists who was eligible to apply. Companies and bodies corporate are right there, next to banks, insurers and non bank lenders. The R.E.C. document describes the wallet as one opened by the issuer or the investor with their own bank. So on paper, the answer is yes.

What I couldn't find is any bank publicly offering these wallets to companies as investors. And these are private placements, offered to investors who are specifically approached. So I think the group is closed in practice, not in the rules. It's closed by who's set up, and by who gets the call. And I think that's most of the reason the L&T bond went to four names.

So what stands between a treasury and this market isn't the regulator, or a rating, or even the technology. It's one wallet with our own bank, and being on the list when the next issue comes round.

So what's the catch, and how long will this take?

The first catch is getting out. There's no trading in these bonds yet. Until the second stage of the pilot arrives, selling means a transfer to another demat account on request, with the money moving outside the same day settlement. The head of NSDL put it plainly. These are for investors, not traders. For now, they're bonds you buy to hold.

The second is price. Business Standard reported that the R.E.C. bond was priced in line with the company's existing bonds that already trade in the market. So there's no premium for being new, and no discount either. Being early buys readiness, not yield.

And then there's time. Nobody has put a date on the second stage, and the NSDL head said moving it into full production needs acceptance, maturity and regulation. The Reserve Bank's wholesale digital rupee pilot started in November twenty twenty two, and the retail one a month later. Almost four years on, both are still called pilots, with no date for a full launch, and the Reserve Bank has said it never meant to roll out a currency all at once. The retail version has actually shrunk, by about a quarter in the year to March. So I wouldn't plan around a date. I'd plan around being ready whenever it comes.

So what would I actually do? Three things, and none of them costs much.

First, I'd ask our relationship bank for a wholesale digital rupee wallet, as an investor, and if the answer is not yet, I'd ask when. I'd rather be on its list than read about the next issue after it's closed.

Second, I'd get the other half ready. That means registering for Demat two-point-oh through our depository participant, and telling the banks and arrangers we use for bond placements that we'd like to be approached for tokenised issues. These are private placements, and the invitation matters.

And third, I'd read our investment policy for anything that isn't about the bond itself. Named custody or settlement arrangements, an approved list of instruments, a sign off for anything new. Our own policy might need a line, and that's much easier to add now than in the middle of a bidding window.

One thing worth saying clearly. If our surplus sits in liquid funds and bank deposits, and we don't buy bonds directly, none of this asks anything of us yet. If a fund we hold does take part, that's the fund's plumbing, not ours.

For as long as most of us have been doing this, the gap between paying for a bond and receiving it was simply part of how the market worked. This pilot shows it doesn't have to be. It's small, it's slow, and for now it's closed in practice. But the rules already let us in. When it opens up, I think the treasuries that get in first will be the ones that already have the wallet.
